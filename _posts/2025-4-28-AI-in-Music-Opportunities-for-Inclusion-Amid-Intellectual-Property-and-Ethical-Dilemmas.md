# AI in Music: Opportunities for Inclusion Amid Intellectual Property and Ethical Dilemmas

*(This Paper was presented at the International Conference on "Navigating Intellectual Property Landscape in the Age of Artificial Intelligence", organized by Faculty of Law, University of Delhi in collaboration with Project Saksham 3.0, held on 26th April 2025)*

## Abstract

This article explores the transformative role of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in the music industry, highlighting its potential for revitalizing endangered languages, enhancing education, supporting mental well-being and empowering marginalized communities. It critically analyses the significant intellectual property rights challenges arising from AI in music creation, including copyright infringement, authorship ambiguity and ethical concerns like cultural appropriation. By examining legal grey areas and shortcomings, this article aims to provide a comprehensive overview and proposes potential solutions such as regulatory reforms and clearer legal frameworks to balance AI innovation with the protection of creators' rights and cultural heritage.


## Introduction

This article examines the transformative role of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in music, highlighting its potential to revitalize endangered indigenous languages in India through AI-generated songs, enhancing educational outcomes for children by creating culturally relevant content and boosting engagement, contributing to mental wellness with personalized and culturally resonant therapeutic music and empowering LGBTQ+ communities by democratizing music production and supporting mental health. The article then critically analyzes the significant intellectual property rights challenges arising from AI's increasing involvement in music creation, including copyright infringement in AI training, the ambiguity of authorship and ownership, and liability issues. It also addresses concerns about cultural appropriation, ethical risks and the impact on human musicians' livelihoods. Finally, this research delves into legal grey areas and proposes potential solutions such as regulatory reforms, clearer copyright frameworks, protection for musicians and indigenous music and enhanced transparency.


## AI Music in Reviving Endangered Indigenous Languages and Empowering Tribal and Indigenous Communities

India, with over 19,500 languages and dialects,[^1] is facing a linguistic crisis. According to UNESCO’s Atlas of the World’s Languages in Danger, India has the highest number of endangered languages globally—170 currently at risk.[^2] Over 220 languages have gone extinct in the past 50 years, and another 150 could vanish within the next five decades.[^3] Extinct languages like Ahom and Bo, and critically endangered ones like Koro and Asur, reflect the urgent need for preservation. As part of the International Decade of Indigenous Languages (2022–2032), AI-generated music presents a powerful tool for revitalizing endangered languages.

The capabilities of AI in revitalising languages on the verge of extinction were highlighted by the Prime Minister of India in a Mann-Ki-Baat episode [^4]. He cited a teacher from Telangana, who used AI tools to compose songs in the endangered Kolami language. These compositions gained traction on social media, showcasing how AI can generate interest in such languages. This aligns with a study on Chinese Manchu ethnic music[^5], which highlighted AI's capabilities in preserving and promoting ethnic music. The research involved creating a high-quality Manchu music dataset, demonstrating AI’s effectiveness in classification, generation and emotion recognition in cultural contexts. It underscores that AI enables efficient documentation, analysis and generation of traditional music forms, aiding digital preservation and accessibility.

Beyond preservation, AI-enhanced audiovisual content in native languages may support early education and strengthen cultural identity among tribal children. AI may also foster inter and intra tribal collaboration by establishing common ground, encouraging risk- taking, driving group momentum, easing friction and reshaping roles[^6]. This strengthens fraternity among these communities, a value embedded in the Constitution of India. Moreover, AI music may also help reclaim indigenous identity suppressed during colonial periods, enabling communities to assert their heritage in the digital age—supporting sovereignty, identity, and self-determination.


## AI Music in Improving Educational Outcomes for Children

Music has long been recognized as a powerful tool in education, improving retention, enhancing communication, emotional development, and academic growth. Music boosts memory retention by pairing information with rhythm and melody. Billy Joel’s “We Didn’t Start the Fire” helped millions recall decades of history effortlessly. Similarly, Vietnam POW Douglas Hegdahl memorized 256 fellow captives’ details by setting them to “Old MacDonald,” proving music’s power as a mnemonic tool. Research also shows that musical engagement improves brain processing, speech perception, reading skills, and classroom atmosphere. Calming music can reduce anxiety, aggression, and stress, fostering emotional regulation and better learning outcomes.[^7]

Artificial Intelligence (AI) amplifies these benefits. AI-generated music tools allow educators to create short, context-specific audio-visual pieces, enriching lessons across subjects.[^8] These tools also address a key barrier to equitable education: the scarcity of culturally and linguistically relevant content, which is particularly applicable to India where there are a multitude of languages but educational content is primarily available only in English and sometimes in other major vernaculars but rarely in languages that are endangered. Children from marginalized linguistic communities often suffer due to hindered comprehension and retention. Examples like the one highlighted in the Mann ki Baat episode (see previous section) show that such AI creations serve two purposes: they can not only create educational content in such languages but can also encode them in catchy jingles. Together, these may help in improving the retention rates in schools along with learning outcomes.

AI music also enhances classroom engagement. A 2022 study, AI as Social Glue [^9], found that AI in collaborative music-making fosters common ground, reduces creative friction, and enhances teamwork—benefits transferable to group learning environments. AI music can also assist in language acquisition by generating songs that help memorize vocabulary—particularly in endangered languages.


## Role of AI and Music in Transforming Mental Wellness

Scientific research increasingly supports music’s therapeutic potential, showing it can trigger dopamine release [^10], enhance mental wellbeing, reduce stress and cortisol levels [^11], and boost cognitive function [^12]. High-frequency music has proven especially effective, elevating oxytocin while decreasing stress hormones.[^13] A Yale study [^14] highlights that music mindfulness—combining listening with mindfulness exercises— targets neural and cardiac mechanisms, effectively treating anxiety and depression. Music therapy is now supported by digital technology.[^15] Apps and platforms offer personalized sound experiences to aid sleep, focus, and emotional regulation. Studies confirm that personalization enhances the effectiveness of music interventions, which are now accessible beyond clinical settings.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is advancing these efforts by tailoring music to individual needs and bio-physiological responses. Algorithms create evidence-based playlists to reduce heart rate and stress, while wearable devices enable real-time adjustment. Companies like LUCID use therapeutic AI to ease anxiety in older adults by adapting to their emotional and musical preferences and providing tailored music [^16]. Soundverse develops tools like “Text to Music” to generate personalized tracks aligned with mental health goals. AI voice assistants, such as SAAR, fine-tune music by adjusting rhythms and harmonies for emotional impact.[^17]

AI also supports culturally resonant therapy, enhancing emotional connection in marginalized communities. Research shows that music embedded with familiar instruments, rhythms, and languages can serve as an effective, affordable therapeutic
tool. Training AI on culturally relevant datasets improves its efficacy in stress relief and emotional support.[^18]


## Role of AI-Generated Music in Empowering LGBTQ+ Communities

Music has long served as a vital platform for advocacy and solidarity within LGBTQ+ communities, fostering identity, resistance, and collective empowerment. As the music industry undergoes a digital transformation, AI-generated music presents new tools for inclusivity and expression.

AI democratizes music production by enabling high-quality creation without expensive studio resources—particularly beneficial for LGBTQ+ artists often marginalized in traditional industry structures. AI tools can generate music that reflects personal narratives and emotional states, offering LGBTQ+ individuals new avenues for identity exploration and self-expression.

Bias in music discovery algorithms on platforms historically sidelined underrepresented voices. However, training AI models on diverse datasets can promote fairer visibility for LGBTQ+ artists.[^19] Moreover, AI-generated music can incorporate culturally specific elements—ancestral rhythms, traditional instruments, and native languages—fostering emotional resonance within intersectional identities.

Additionally, AI-generated soundscapes and personalized compositions contribute to mental health support by addressing stress and emotional regulation, critical given the discrimination LGBTQ+ individuals often face.[^20]


## The Indispensable Rights of Music Creators and Legal Safeguards within the IPR Regime

The ability of music creators to earn a living is fundamentally supported by legal protections under intellectual property law, particularly copyright and related rights. These rights grant creators control over the use and commercial exploitation of their works.

Copyright protection applies to original musical works, including compositions (melody and harmony) and lyrics, once fixed in a tangible form. In India, the Copyright Act of 1957 governs these rights, while internationally, the Berne Convention ensures automatic protection upon creation. Copyright grants creators exclusive economic rights, including the rights to reproduce, distribute, publicly perform, broadcast, and adapt their works. These rights are central to monetization in the music industry.

Related rights (neighboring rights) protect performers, producers of phonograms (sound recordings), and broadcasting organizations. Performers hold rights to authorize or prohibit the recording, broadcasting, and distribution of their performances and are entitled to equitable remuneration. Producers and record labels, who invest in production and dissemination, hold rights over the reproduction and distribution of phonograms. Broadcasters control the rebroadcast and copying of their transmissions.

Moral rights, as outlined in Article 6bis of the Berne Convention, are personal rights granted to creators. These include the right of attribution (paternity) and the right to object to derogatory treatment (integrity). Such rights protect the creator’s reputation and are generally non-transferable.

Collective Management Organizations (CMOs) (like JASRAC APRA, AMCOS) manage copyright and related rights on behalf of creators. They license music for various uses, collect royalties, and distribute payments, enabling creators to manage their rights in complex digital environments.

Together, these rights form the legal backbone of economic and moral protection for music creators across the global industry.


## Recent Legal Cases Involving AI in the Music Industry and Intellectual Property Rights

The rise of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in music creation has triggered complex legal challenges in intellectual property rights (IPR). As generative AI systems increasingly produce music that mimics existing works, traditional copyright and authorship frameworks are being tested globally.

A key legal issue involves the unauthorized use of copyrighted material—lyrics, compositions, and sound recordings—for AI training. In India, major Bollywood labels including T-Series and Saregama, under the Indian Music Industry (IMI), have joined a copyright suit against OpenAI. They allege that OpenAI's ChatGPT unlawfully used sound recordings during training, constituting copyright infringement. OpenAI has contested the suit, citing lack of jurisdiction as a U.S.-based entity. This case emphasizes the tension between AI data training requirements and creators’ rights.

In the U.S., Concord Music Group & Ors. v. Anthropic PBC[^21] saw publishers UMG, Concord, and ABKCO sue AI developer Anthropic in 2023 for infringing lyrics of over 500 songs. While the plaintiffs argued that this undermines licensing markets, the court did not rule on fair use, noting that the legality of licensing AI training data remains unsettled.

Similarly, the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA) filed lawsuits against Suno and Udio[^22] —AI platforms accused of training on massive copyrighted catalogs without authorization. In UMG v. Suno (Massachusetts) and UMG v. Udio (New York), it was alleged that their outputs closely resemble copyrighted works such as Chuck Berry’s “Johnny B. Goode” and The Temptations’ “My Girl.” Defendants claimed their training practices fall under fair use and produce non-infringing music. The RIAA counters that these services engage in “imitative machine-generated music,” not protected creative expression.
 
Ownership of AI-generated content also presents legal ambiguity. In India, the AI painting tool “RAGHAV” was briefly granted co-authorship, but this was withdrawn by the Copyright Office.[^23] Citing Sections 2(d)(iii) and (iv) of the Copyright Act, it was reaffirmed that authorship requires human involvement.

In China, the 2019 Tencent v. Yingxun case[^24] involved an article created by Tencent’s Dreamwriter AI. The Nanshan District People’s Court ruled it a copyrightable literary work due to significant human input in its creation—data formatting, trigger design, and algorithmic proofreading—thereby meeting the originality threshold.

Fair use (US) and fair dealing (India, UK, Canada) are central in these debates. In Andersen v. Stability AI Ltd.[^25], artists in the U.S. sued over AI image generators trained on copyrighted visuals. Courts have yet to determine if AI processes qualify as transformative use under fair use. India’s Parliament clarified that any commercial AI use of copyrighted work requires explicit rights-holder consent under the Copyright Act, 1957[^26].

Personality rights have also come under scrutiny. In Arijit Singh v. Codible Ventures LLP[^27], the Bombay High Court ruled in favor of the Indian singer, whose voice was cloned via AI without consent. The court held this unauthorized commercial use violated his publicity and personality rights, potentially harming his career. The defendants were restrained from any further infringement.

These cases underscore the urgent need for updated global legal frameworks to protect intellectual property in the evolving AI-driven music industry and bring parity in the rights of the stakeholders involved worldover.

## Legal Grey Areas and Challenges from Artificial Intelligence in the Music Industry

Despite existing legal protections, the rapid advancement of AI in music has exposed several legal grey areas and systemic shortcomings.

*Authorship and Ownership:* Traditional copyright law is based on human authorship, creating uncertainty when AI is involved in music creation. India's Copyright Act recognizes “the person who causes the work to be created” as the author of computer- generated works. However, the case of AI tool RAGHAV—initially granted and later denied co-authorship—demonstrates this ambiguity. Similarly, in the DABUS case, South Africa accepted AI as an inventor, but the US, UK, and EPO rejected the claim, affirming that inventors must be human. [^28] Globally, it remains unclear whether copyright in AI- generated works vests in the programmer, user, the program or no one at all.

*Copyright Infringement in AI Training:* AI systems often train on vast datasets that may include copyrighted music, frequently without permission. This has triggered lawsuits, such as those by the RIAA against Suno AI and Udio AI, challenging the application of fair use. Rights holders argue for licensing frameworks and opt-out mechanisms to regulate the use of copyrighted content in AI training.

*Liability for Infringement:* When AI generates infringing content, it is unclear who is liable—the AI developer, the model trainer or the end user. Existing laws do not adequately assign responsibility in such scenarios.

*Cultural Appropriation and Ethical Risks:* Especially a concern for Indigenous creators. 89% of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander and 76% of Māori artists fear AI will commodify and misrepresent cultural traditions[^29]. Indigenous African music, rooted in oral tradition and communal ownership, is especially vulnerable to unauthorized use.[^30] AI may ingest such works without consent, reducing them to data and perpetuating historical exploitation. Current copyright laws, focused on individual ownership, often fail to protect these community owned and generally orally transmitted expressions. These issues are relevant to India, given its indigenous diversity and similar gaps in legal frameworks for traditional cultural works.

*Impact on Human Creators:* AI’s efficiency in creating music threatens human musicians’ livelihoods, particularly in commercial sectors. By 2028, 24% of global music and 21% of audiovisual revenues may be lost to AI-generated content[^31]. In Australia and New Zealand alone, creators could lose over half a billion AUD.[^32] Despite this, no remuneration systems currently exist for affected artists.

*Transparency and Credit:* Artists deserve disclosure of whether their works were used in AI training and proper attribution when AI exploits their content. The absence of such obligations is a significant shortcoming.

*Visibility and Authenticity:* AI-generated music may dominate recommendation algorithms, reducing visibility of human-made content and making it particularly difficult for budding artists to find an audience and establish themselves. It is also generally argued that such music lacks emotional depth, devaluing human creativity and originality.


## Suggested Solutions

To address the multifaceted challenges arising from AI in music creation, a comprehensive legal, ethical, cultural, and technological framework is needed.

*Regulatory and Policy Reforms:* Governments should adopt EU-style transparency guidelines requiring tech companies to disclose copyrighted content used in AI training, with penalties for non-compliance. Although the official stand of the Indian government is that the present legal framework is sufficient to tackle AI related challenges[^33] but, given the fast paced nature of AI and its limitless applications, there is a need for tailored policies in this area. Global copyright frameworks must align with instruments like the UNDRIP, ensuring consent and fair compensation to the indigenous. Legislative efforts should encourage AI innovation while protecting human creators and ensuring fair compensation.

*Copyright and Ownership Clarity:* Clear legal frameworks must define ownership and attribution in AI-generated music. Recognition should be given to both human and AI contributions, with customizable attribution tools and licensing transparency. Given the collaborative nature of AI-human co-creation, flexible copyright systems are essential to allocate rights and royalties fairly.

*Protection of Musicians’ Livelihoods:* Fair remuneration mechanisms are vital for creators whose work is used in AI training. Mandatory compensation systems for generative AI, potentially through blockchain-based tracking, should be considered. Cross-sector cooperation involving record labels, publishers, CMOs, and platforms is crucial to build sustainable revenue models.

*Safeguarding Indigenous Music and Cultural Integrity:* Indigenous musical works, deeply tied to cultural heritage, spirituality, and communal identity, may qualify as Traditional Knowledge (TK) and Traditional Cultural Expressions. Under India’s proposed Protection of TK Bill 2022[^34], such music may get legal protection. Such sui generis legal protections are the need of the hour. Community-driven documentation, ethical licensing, and transparent datasets should support Indigenous ownership and control, as exemplified by the San Code of Research Ethics[^35].

*Transparency and Disclosure:* AI companies must disclose use of copyrighted data and label AI-generated content. Streaming platforms should flag algorithmically curated playlists and ensure transparency in recommender systems.

*Enhancing Visibility of Human-Made Music:* Platforms should prioritize human-made music. Music recommender systems must avoid bias and promote genre diversity. Algorithmic decision-making should be observable and critically examined.

*Additional Considerations:* Education for music creators and transparency in AI development—via principles like “show the work” from data feminism[^36]—are essential to recognize and value creative labor.


## Conclusion:

In conclusion, this article highlights AI’s transformative role in music—revitalizing languages, enhancing education, supporting well-being, and empowering marginalized communities. However, it also challenges legal frameworks, especially around intellectual property, authorship, and unauthorized use. Addressing cultural appropriation and ensuring fair compensation for human creators is essential. Sustainable progress requires regulatory reforms, clear legal definitions, and ethical guidelines to balance innovation with protection of creators' rights and cultural heritage. Proactive measures are needed to harness AI’s potential. "Mile sur mera tumhara" now echoes human-AI harmony, composing the inclusive symphony, “Hamara sur”, of tomorrow.
