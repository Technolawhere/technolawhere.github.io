# R.G. Anand v. Delux Films and Ors. (1978 INSC 138) (AIR 1978 SC 1613)
### Supreme Court of India | Civil Appeal No. 2030 of 1968 | Decided on 18.08.1978
**Coram:** Raja Jaswant Singh, R.S. Pathak and S. Murtaza Fazal Ali, JJ.

---

## 1. One-Minute Summary

The appellant, R.G. Anand, an architect and playwright, wrote and staged a Hindi play titled *"Hum Hindustani"* in 1954, built around the theme of provincialism and its effect on an inter-provincial marriage proposal between a Punjabi and a Madrasi family. After discussions between the appellant and the second respondent (a film director) about the possibility of filming the play, the respondents released a motion picture called *"New Delhi"* in 1956. The appellant alleged that the film was a colourable imitation of his play and sued for damages, accounts of profits, and a permanent injunction, claiming violation of his copyright under the Copyright Act, 1911.

Both the trial court (District Judge, Delhi) and the Delhi High Court held that the appellant owned the copyright in the play, but that the film did not infringe it. The Supreme Court, hearing the matter on concurrent findings of fact, dismissed the appeal.

**Ratio decidendi:** Copyright does not subsist in an idea, theme, plot, or historical/legendary fact. It only subsists in the particular form of expression given to that idea. Where two works share a common theme, similarities are inevitable, but such similarities do not by themselves establish infringement unless they extend to the substantial and material form, structure, and manner of expression of the copyrighted work. The surest test is whether an ordinary spectator or reader, after seeing/reading both works, gets an unmistakable impression that the subsequent work is a copy of the original. Where broad and material dissimilarities exist alongside superficial similarities and where those dissimilarities negative any intention to copy, there is no infringement.

**The "seven propositions" (para 52):** The Court distilled the case law into seven numbered propositions covering:
    (i) no copyright in ideas/themes/plots/historical facts;
    (ii) the need to assess whether similarities extend to fundamental/substantial aspects of expression;
    (iii) the "surest test" of the impression on an ordinary spectator/reader;
    (iv) no infringement where a common theme is treated differently so as to produce a new work;
    (v) no infringement where material dissimilarities negative an intention to copy;
    (vi) the requirement of clear and cogent evidence; and
    (vii) the heightened difficulty of proof where a stage play is alleged to have been converted into a film.
  These propositions have functioned as a structured checklist for subsequent Indian courts assessing copyright infringement claims.


**Final Decision:** Appeal dismissed; no order as to costs in the Supreme Court.

## 2. Statutory Provisions

| Provision | Text/Substance | Relevance in Judgment |
|---|---|---|
| **Copyright Act, 1911, Section 1(2)** | Defines "copyright" as the sole right to produce or reproduce a work (or a substantial part of it) in any material form, to perform it in public, and, if unpublished, to publish it. | The Court relied on this definition to establish the scope of the exclusive rights conferred on the copyright owner, and specifically invoked **Section 1(2)(d)**, which extends copyright in a literary, dramatic, or musical work to the right to make a "record, perforated roll, cinematograph film, or other contrivance" by which the work may be mechanically performed; the provision under which the appellant's play (a dramatic work) was held protected. |
| **Copyright Act, 1911, Section 2(1)** | Provides that copyright in a work is infringed by any person who, without the owner's consent, does anything which the sole right to do is conferred on the copyright owner by the Act. | Formed the statutory basis for examining whether the respondents' film infringed the appellant's exclusive rights in the play. |

The Court noted that at the time the cause of action arose, Parliament had not yet enacted an Indian copyright statute (the case predates the Copyright Act, 1957 in terms of the applicable law), so the courts applied the British Copyright Act, 1911.

## 3. Facts and Background

- The appellant, R.G. Anand, was an architect by profession and also a playwright/producer of stage plays, having earlier written and produced plays such as *Des Hamara*, *Azadi*, and *Election*.
- In 1953, he wrote the Hindi play *"Hum Hindustani"*, first staged in February 1954 at Wavell Theatre, New Delhi, under the auspices of the Indian National Theatre. The play was well received by the press and public and was re-staged multiple times through 1954–1956, including at Calcutta.
- In November 1954, the second respondent, Mr. Mohan Sehgal (a film director), wrote to the appellant seeking a copy of the play, having earlier received a synopsis from a common friend, Mr. Balwant Gargi, to consider making a film based on it.
- The appellant declined to send a copy but invited the respondents to see the play staged in Delhi in December 1954.
- Around January 1955, the second and third respondents met the appellant in Delhi, where the appellant read out and explained the entire play to them. No firm commitment to film the play was made at that meeting.
- In May 1955, the second respondent announced production of a film titled *"New Delhi"*. The appellant was informed by a member of his own cast that the film in production was based on his play. The appellant wrote expressing concern; the respondent assured him that the film's story, treatment, characters, etc. were entirely different.
- The film was released in Delhi in September 1956. After viewing it, the appellant concluded that the film was substantially based on his play and filed suit for damages, accounts of profits, and a permanent injunction restraining exhibition of the film.
- **Trial Court findings:** The District Judge held (i) that the appellant owned the copyright in the play, but (ii) that the film "New Delhi" did not infringe that copyright.
- **High Court:** The Delhi High Court, in appeal, affirmed the District Court's decree in its entirety.
- **Before the Supreme Court:** The appellant did not challenge the findings of fact but argued that the legal principles applied by the courts below to determine infringement were incorrect and inconsistent with settled law in England, America, and India.

---

## 4. Legal Issues

Framed by the trial court and carried through to the Supreme Court, the central issues were:

1. **Regarding Ownership:** Was the appellant the owner of the copyright in the play *"Hum Hindustani"*? *(Decided in the appellant's favour by the trial court and not contested thereafter.)*
2. **Regarding Infringement:** Was the film *"New Delhi"* an infringement of the appellant's copyright in the play?
3. **Regarding Liability:** Had the respondents (or any of them) infringed the appellant's copyright by producing, distributing, or exhibiting the film?
4. A subsidiary procedural issue regarding misjoinder of parties and cause of action, which was not pressed by the respondents and decided against them.

Before the Supreme Court, the substantive question was recast as a legal one: **what are the correct principles for determining whether a cinematographic film constitutes a violation of copyright subsisting in a stage play**, and whether those principles were correctly applied to the facts as found by the courts below.

---

## 5. Analysis of Each Legal Issue

### Issue 1 & the threshold question: Is there copyright in an idea, theme, or subject matter?

**Arguments:**
- The appellant's counsel argued that the similarities between the play and the film were so close and numerous as to lead to the irresistible inference that the film was an imitation of the play, and that the courts below had misapplied the governing legal principles.
- The respondents argued that the two courts below had correctly applied the law, that there was a vast difference in spirit and content between the play and the film, and that no violation of copyright arose merely because both works shared "provincialism" as a common theme.

**Court's reasoning:**
The Court held, drawing extensively on English and American authority as well as scholarly commentary, that **copyright does not extend to ideas, themes, plots, or subject matter. It extends only to the particular form or expression given to them**. The Court invoked the following authorities on this point:

- **Halsbury's Laws of England (4th Edn.)**: cited for the proposition that copyright is not concerned with originality of ideas but with the expression of thought, and that copyright subsists in the dramatic incidents created, not the abstract situation.
- **Copinger on Copyright (11th Edn.)**: relied upon for the principle that what is protected is "the original expression of thought or information in some concrete form," and that a defendant is not liable merely for taking the essential idea and expressing it in his own form.
- **Corpus Juris Secundum, Vol. 18**: used to establish that an author has a common-law property right in his intellectual production, and that infringement/piracy consists of doing, without consent, anything which the statute reserves solely to the copyright owner.
- **American Jurisprudence**: cited for the proposition that the law does not recognise property rights in an abstract idea; an idea becomes protectable only once given embodiment in tangible form.
- **Donoghue v. Allied Newspapers (1937)3 All E.R. 503** (Farwell, J.): applied for the settled proposition that there is no copyright in an idea until it is reduced to writing or some tangible form; copyright exists only in the particular form of language or expression used to convey the idea.
- **Harman Pictures N.V. v. Osborne (1967) 1 W.L.R. 723** (Goff, J.): distinguished ideas, schemes, and systems (not copyrightable) from situations and incidents (which may be), while cautioning against inferring copying merely from similarity of "stock incidents" traceable to common historical or literary sources.
- **Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp. 81 F.2d 40** (Judge Learned Hand): cited for the view that others may copy the "theme" or "idea" of a work but not its "expression," and that unconscious plagiarism is actionable as much as deliberate plagiarism, though on the facts of that American case no infringement was found.
- **Shipman v. R.K.O. Radio Pictures 100 F.2d 533**: cited to show that courts assess infringement by reference to the impression conveyed to the audience, since ideas are not copyrightable but the sequence of events may be.

The Court also invoked, by way of illustration, Shakespeare's plays — based on Greek-Roman and British mythology and legendary sources. Done to demonstrate that a dramatist may take a well-worn theme and, through original treatment, style, and expression, produce an entirely new copyrightable work without infringing any prior work on the same theme.

**Application to facts:** The central theme common to both the play and the film, i.e., provincialism/parochialism, particularly its effect on inter-provincial marriage, was held to be an idea/theme incapable of monopolisation by the appellant. Any resemblance flowing purely from this shared theme could not, by itself, found a claim of infringement.

---

### Issue 2: What test determines whether the *expression* of a common idea has been unlawfully copied?

**Court's reasoning — the "surest test":**
Relying on **Hanfstaengl v. W.H. Smith & Sons (1905) 1 Ch. D. 519** (Bayley, J.'s formulation, as approved via *West v. Francis* (1822)), the Court adopted the test that **"a copy is that which comes so near to the original as to suggest the original to the mind of every person seeing it."** This was elevated into the Court's own formulation: if, after seeing/reading both works, a person forms a definite opinion and dominant impression that the subsequent work is based on the original, infringement is established.

This was reinforced by:
- **Ladbroke (Football) Ltd. v. William Hill (Football) Ltd. (1964) 1 All E.R. 465** (Lord Reid, Lord Hodson): for the principle that a copyright work must be assessed as a whole, and one should not dissect it into fragments to test each fragment's copyrightability individually; the correct approach is to ask whether the work as a whole is original and protected, and then whether the part allegedly taken is *substantial*.
- **Corelli v. Gray, 29 T.L.R. 570** (Sargent, J.): for the "four hypotheses" analysis of similarities (mere chance; common source; plaintiff copying defendant; or defendant copying plaintiff) and that the defendant would be protected in the first 3 situations and the plaintiff would only succeed if the last situaton is met, and the principle that the cumulative, aggregate effect of similarities, not any single similarity in isolation, may justify an inference of copying.
- **Hawkes & Son (London) Ltd. v. Paramount Film Service Ltd. [1934] 1 Ch. D. 593**: applied to show that even a short but "vital and essential" reproduction of a copyrighted musical work (there, a recognisable march) could constitute substantial taking.
- **Twentieth Century Fox Film Corp. v. Stonesifer, 140 F.2d 579**: cited for the proposition that the two works "should be considered and tested, not hypercritically or with meticulous scrutiny, but by the observations and impressions of the average reasonable reader and spectator," and that numerous striking similarities, if not attributable to mere chance, support an inference of substantial unlawful copying.
- **Otto Eisenschiml v. Fawcett Publications, 246 F.2d 598**: for the principle that infringement is not confined to literal reproduction but extends to imitation or paraphrasing carried to a sufficient extent, and that the inquiry is one of quality and value, not mere quantity.
- Indian precedents applying the same "impression" test to artistic works: **K.R. Venugopalan Sarma v. Sangu Ganesan, 1972 Cr. L.J. 1098**; **The Daily Calendar Supplying Bureau, Sivakasi v. The United Concern, MANU/TN/0256/1967**; and **C. Cunniah & Co. v. Balraj & Co., MANU/TN/0167/1961** — each holding that the degree of resemblance must be such that a viewer of the alleged copy gets the suggestion that it is the original.

**Distinguishing/limiting authority:** The Court noted **Bobbs-Merrill Co. v. Straus, 210 U.S. 339** and other US decisions largely for supporting general principles rather than for contrary holdings; and cited **Frederick B. Chatterton v. Joseph Arnold Cave (1878) 3 A.C. 483** (Hatherley) for the *de minimis* principle (that where the quantity or quality taken is neither substantial nor material, no actionable wrong arises, even in the case of dramatic works).

---

### Issue 3: What is the burden and standard of proof, particularly where a stage play is alleged to have been adapted into a film?

**Court's reasoning:**
The Court held that violation of copyright, being in the nature of an act of piracy, **must be proved by clear and cogent evidence**, applying the various tests discussed. It further held that **the burden becomes more difficult to discharge where the alleged infringement is of a stage play by a film**, because a film has a "much broader perspective, a wider field and a bigger background" allowing the film-maker to introduce a variety of incidents that give the work a different colour and complexion from the copyrighted play, even while still drawing on it.

Supporting authorities:
- **N.T. Raghunathan v. All India Reporter Ltd., MANU/MH/0270/1957** was cited as an instance where copying extended beyond ideas to style of abridgment and expression, justifying a finding of infringement (contrasted with the present case, where no such extensive copying of expression was found).
- **Macmillan & Co. Ltd. v. K. & J. Cooper, 51 I.A. 109** (Lord Atkinson, Privy Council) was cited for the proposition that piracy requires either substantial copying or such imitation as amounts to a mere evasion of copyright.
- **Deeks v. H.G. Wells & Ors., 60 I.A. 26** (Lord Atkin, Privy Council) was cited for the principle that intrinsic evidence of copying, though possible, must be of the most cogent force before being accepted against credible direct testimony denying copying.
- **Mohini Mohan Singh v. Sita Nath Basak, A.I.R. 1931 Cal. 230** was cited for reinforcing that mere similarity is not enough, since similarity may result from any of several hypotheses (chance, common source, or copying in either direction), and that each case turns on its own facts.

**Application to facts:** The Court noted that the second respondent admitted having heard the entire play narrated by the appellant before the film was made, and admitted being "aware" of the story and "to some extent inspired" by it. However, there was no reliable evidence that the respondent had ever expressed an intention to film the appellant's specific play, and the respondents maintained that they were independently searching for a story on the theme of provincialism.

---

### Issue 4: Application of the tests to the specific similarities and dissimilarities between the play and the film

**The appellant's 18 pleaded similarities** (para 9 of the plaint) included matters such as: the shared theme of provincialism; the depiction of two families (Punjabi and Madrasi); a shared character name ("Subramaniam"/"Subramanyam") for the Madrasi father; a Delhi locale; disruption of family cordiality upon discovery of a cross-provincial love affair; the girl's fondness for music/dance; a cowardly hero unable to confront his parents; a suicide note; and reconciliation of the families following the shock of an (attempted) suicide.

**Court's evaluation:** The Court, having itself heard the play read out and viewed the film, agreed with the trial court's assessment that these similarities were **"trivial," "trifling," and "insignificant,"** and were adequately explained by the shared central idea of provincialism, which is not protectable. The mere coincidence of a common name (Subramaniam) was held immaterial, as personal names are common property and cannot found a copyright claim.

**Material dissimilarities identified by the Court** included:
- In the play, provincialism surfaces only in the marriage context; in the film, it is the story's starting point (the hero's search for lodging), an entirely new theme not developed in the play.
- In the play, both families know each other's identities throughout; in the film, the hero's identity is concealed until a late dramatic disclosure (a materially different treatment of the same idea).
- The play features a joint suicide pact by both lovers with a note; the film features only the heroine's solitary suicide attempt, thwarted differently, followed by a different chain of events (including her being disguised as a "niece").
- The play involves only two families; the film introduces a third (Bengali) family and subplot, central to the film's resolution.
- The film additionally depicts two social evils entirely absent from the play, the evils of a caste-ridden society and the evil of the dowry system, which the Court held were "important and substantial parts of the story," not mere embellishments.

**Conclusion on this issue:** The dissimilarities were held to "far outweigh" the similarities. The Court held that **no prudent person, after seeing the play and the film, would form the impression that the film is a copy of the play**, and that at most the central (unprotectable) idea of provincialism was common to both.

---

## 6. Decision

- The Supreme Court held that the appellant had **not proved, by clear and cogent evidence, that the respondents committed a colourable imitation of the play** or thereby violated the copyright of the appellant.
- The concurrent findings of fact by the trial court and the High Court (that the dissimilarities between the play and the film were material and outweighed the similarities) were upheld; the Supreme Court declined to disturb them.
- **The appeal was dismissed, with no order as to costs in the Supreme Court.**
- Raja Jaswant Singh, J. concurred fully with the judgment of Fazal Ali, J.
- R.S. Pathak, J., while concurring in the result (given the reluctance of the Supreme Court to interfere with concurrent findings of fact), wrote separately to caution that in a "clearer case," the Court may need to guard against copyright being evaded through immaterial changes, insubstantial differences, and an artificially widened scope of theme designed to create a false impression of dissimilarity.

---

## 7. Legal Significance

- **Idea–expression dichotomy authoritatively affirmed in Indian law:** The judgment is the foundational Indian Supreme Court authority for the principle that copyright subsists only in the *expression* of an idea, theme, or subject matter, and not in the idea itself.
- **The "seven propositions" (para 52):** The Court distilled the case law into seven numbered propositions covering:
    (i) no copyright in ideas/themes/plots/historical facts;
    (ii) the need to assess whether similarities extend to fundamental/substantial aspects of expression;
    (iii) the "surest test" of the impression on an ordinary spectator/reader;
    (iv) no infringement where a common theme is treated differently so as to produce a new work;
    (v) no infringement where material dissimilarities negative an intention to copy;
    (vi) the requirement of clear and cogent evidence; and
    (vii) the heightened difficulty of proof where a stage play is alleged to have been converted into a film.
  These propositions have functioned as a structured checklist for subsequent Indian courts assessing copyright infringement claims.
- **The "impression test":** By adopting and restating the *Hanfstaengl* formulation (whether the accused work would suggest the original to the mind of an ordinary observer) the Court entrenched a holistic, non-dissective method of comparison (echoing *Ladbroke v. William Hill*) as opposed to a mechanical, line-by-line similarity count.
- **Cumulative assessment of similarities and dissimilarities:** The judgment emphasises weighing the totality of similarities against the totality of dissimilarities, rather than resting a finding of infringement on isolated points of resemblance: a methodology later applied broadly in Indian film/literary copyright disputes.
- **Special treatment of stage-play-to-film adaptation claims:** The Court's recognition that a film's broader canvas allows a defendant to introduce substantial new material while still drawing on a copyrighted play, thereby making the plaintiff's burden of proof more difficult, has been a recurring reference point in disputes over unauthorised film adaptations of literary or dramatic works.
- **A note of caution (Pathak, J.):** The separate observations of Pathak, J., that courts must remain alert to attempts at "colourable imitation" disguised through immaterial changes and an artificially enlarged scope, have been cited as a counterweight to the majority's otherwise defendant-favourable outcome, underscoring that the substantial-similarity test is not a license for superficial alteration to defeat legitimate copyright claims.
