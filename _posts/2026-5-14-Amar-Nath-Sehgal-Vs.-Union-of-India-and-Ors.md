# Amar Nath Sehgal v. Union of India & Ors. (Delhi High Court, 2005)

**Citation:** CS(OS) 2074/1992, (2005) 30 PTC 253 Del
**Court:** Delhi High Court
**Decided on:** 21.02.2005
**Coram:** Pradeep Nandrajog, J.

---

## 1. One-Minute Summary

Sculptor Amar Nath Sehgal was commissioned by the Government of India in 1957 to create a bronze mural for the lobby of Vigyan Bhawan, New Delhi. Over more than five years, he created a 140 ft. by 40 ft. bronze mural depicting India's cultural and scientific heritage. In 1979, the Government pulled the mural down without Sehgal's consent and consigned the remnants to a storeroom, causing significant damage. Sehgal sued under Section 57 of the Copyright Act, 1957, seeking a declaration that his moral rights had been violated, an injunction, damages, and delivery-up of the remnants.

The court framed five issues: limitation, whether the plaintiff had rights under Section 57 despite having assigned copyright to the Government, whether those rights were violated, whether damage was suffered, and relief. The court held the suit was within limitation because the Government's correspondence up to 1991 kept alive the possibility of redress. On the substantive question, the court held that Section 57 protects an author's moral rights of paternity and integrity independently of, and even after assignment of, economic copyright, and that these rights survive destruction of the work — indeed, destruction is the "extreme form of mutilation." The court further held that where a work has attained the status of a nation's cultural heritage, the author's right of integrity extends to protecting the work's integrity in relation to that cultural heritage, drawing on international conventions to which India is a signatory.

**Ratio decidendi:** Section 57 of the Copyright Act, 1957 must be interpreted widely to include destruction of a work of art within "mutilation," since destruction diminishes the volume of the author's creative corpus and is thus prejudicial to his honour and reputation; and where the work has acquired the status of a modern national treasure, the moral right of integrity extends to protection of that cultural heritage.

**Application:** Applying this reasoning, the court found the plaintiff's international eminence and the mural's status as a national treasure established, found the Government's destruction and mishandling of the mural undisputed on the evidence, and held both the plaintiff's personal moral rights and the nation's cultural heritage had been violated.

**Final Decision:** The suit was decreed in the plaintiff's favour — mandatory return of the mural remnants, a declaration vesting all rights in the mural in the plaintiff with an absolute right to recreate and sell it, damages of Rs. 5 lacs with interest, and costs.

**Why it is a landmark:** This is widely regarded as one of the first Indian decisions to give expansive, cultural-heritage-oriented content to the moral right of integrity under Section 57, holding that destruction of a work can itself violate the author's moral rights and that those rights can extend to safeguarding a work's status as national cultural heritage.

## 2. Statutory Provisions

| Provision | Discussion in the Judgment |
|---|---|
| **Section 57, Copyright Act, 1957** (Author's special rights) | The central provision. The judgment traces its original 1957 text — which allowed an author to restrain or claim damages for (a) any distortion, mutilation, or modification of the work, or (b) any other action prejudicial to his honour or reputation — and its post-amendment form (effective 10.5.1995), under which the qualifying words "prejudicial to his honour or reputation" now govern the entire right to restrain or claim damages, and where an explanation was added stating that failure to display a work is not an infringement of the section. The court held that these rights exist independently of the author's copyright and survive even after assignment of that copyright. |
| **Section 52, Copyright Act, 1957** | Referred to only incidentally, via the proviso to the amended Section 57(1), which excludes from the author's special rights any objection to adaptation of a computer programme permitted under Section 52(1)(aa). The provision is not otherwise discussed on its merits, as it was not material to the facts of this case. |

## 3. Facts and Background

- In 1957, the Central Public Works Department, acting on a recommendation of the Government's Decoration Committee, invited Amar Nath Sehgal to execute a wire/bronze sculpture decoration for the foyer of Vigyan Bhawan, then being built to host national and international conferences. The scheme of commissioning Indian artists for public buildings traced back to a note by Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru encouraging such initiatives.
- Sehgal accepted, and after over five years of work produced a bronze mural spanning 140 feet and 40 feet in height, occupying the lobby wall of Vigyan Bhawan, on the theme of rural and modern India.
- The mural remained in place until 1979, when it was pulled down and placed in a Government storeroom without Sehgal's permission, consent, or authorisation, resulting in damage.
- Sehgal made representations to the Government over several years. A Joint Secretary in the Ministry of Human Resource Development observed in 1991 that although Sehgal could be advised simply to sue, the Government ought to deal fairly with the artist's work. A subsequent letter dated 6.6.1991 from a Joint Secretary in the Ministry of Urban Development acknowledged that the mural was "merely dumped in a store" but that its pieces were "largely intact," and offered to reinstall it in the Vigyan Bhawan annexe.
- No effective redress followed, and a legal notice sent by Sehgal on 1.9.1991 went unanswered on its factual contents.
- Sehgal filed suit in 1992 under Section 57 of the Copyright Act seeking: (i) a declaration that his rights under Section 57 were violated, with an apology; (ii) a permanent injunction against further distortion, mutilation, or damage; (iii) Rs. 50 lacs in damages for humiliation, injury, insult, and loss of reputation; and (iv) delivery-up of the mural for restoration at the defendants' cost.
- An interim order dated 29.5.1992 (Jaspal Singh, J.) restrained the defendants from causing further loss or injury to the mural or doing anything prejudicial to the plaintiff's honour or reputation as its author.
- The Union of India defended on the grounds that it owned the mural (having paid the plaintiff for the work) and was entitled to consign it to storage, and separately pleaded that the suit, filed in 1992 in respect of an act occurring in 1979, was barred by limitation.
- Court-appointed experts — including artist B.C. Sanyal, Professor P.N. Mago, and Ms. Kapila Vatsyayan of the Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts — inspected the mural's remnants and reported extensive destruction and missing parts, findings the defendants did not dispute.

---

## 4. Legal Issues

The court framed the following issues on 1.4.2003:

1. Whether the suit is barred by limitation.
2. Whether the plaintiff has rights under Section 57 of the Copyright Act, 1957 in the mural, although copyright in the work vests in the defendants.
3. Whether the defendants violated the plaintiff's rights under Section 57.
4. Whether the plaintiff suffered any damage.
5. Relief.

---

## 5. Analysis of Each Legal Issue

### Issue 1: Limitation

**Defendants' argument:** The cause of action arose in 1979, when the mural was removed and damaged. Since the suit was filed in 1992 — well beyond the three-year limitation period — it was time-barred.

**Plaintiff's argument:** The suit concerned violation of moral rights, which subsist for the author's lifetime and had not been waived. Further, the plaintiff had continued to approach Government functionaries for redressal, and at no point did the Government unequivocally reject his claim; the cause of action therefore could not be treated as having crystallised, and become time-barred, in 1979 itself.

**Court's reasoning:** The court examined the correspondence and found that as late as 6.6.1991, the Government (through Joint Secretary Indrani Sen) acknowledged that the mural "needed respect" and offered to reinstall it, describing the remnants as "largely intact." This letter, though not formally exhibited, was held proved: it had been annexed to the plaintiff's affidavit, was referred to in the plaintiff's legal notice dated 1.9.1991 (whose receipt the defendants admitted), and its factual contents were never rebutted by the defendants. On this basis, the court held that limitation began running only after 6.6.1991, since until that date the defendants had expressed willingness to restore the mural without acting on that commitment.

**Ratio on this issue:** Where a defendant's conduct keeps alive the possibility of redress and does not unequivocally deny the claim, limitation does not run from the date of the original offending act but from the point at which that possibility of redress is foreclosed.

**Finding:** Decided in favour of the plaintiff; the suit was held to be within limitation.

---

### Issue 2 & 3: Whether the Plaintiff Has Rights Under Section 57 Despite Assignment of Copyright, and Whether Those Rights Were Violated

These two issues were dealt with together as they raised a common question of statutory interpretation.

**Evidentiary foundation:** The court first recorded that authorship of the mural by the plaintiff was undisputed. It relied on unrebutted evidence of the plaintiff's international standing — his entry in "Who's Who in the World," display of his work in over 20 countries, the Lalit Kala Academy Award and President's Award, gifts of his sculptures to Presidents Jimmy Carter and Richard Nixon on behalf of the Government of India (with appreciative acknowledgements from both), and recognition by the President of India — to find that the plaintiff enjoyed "celebrity status" as an artist.

**The plaintiff's position:** Counsel for the plaintiff (Shri Praveen Anand) argued for a wide reading of Section 57: that an action under the section need not be confined to injunction or damages but could extend to preserving the ethic of the work, and that "mutilation" within the section should be understood to include outright destruction of a work, since destruction is simply the most extreme degree of mutilation.

**The defendants' position:** The Union of India urged the narrower, contrary view.

**Court's interpretative approach:**

- The court traced the conceptual foundation of moral rights, distinguishing four possible authorial rights: the *paternity/attribution right* (to be identified as author), the *dissemination/divulgation right* (essentially economic, to publish and sell), the *integrity right* (to object to derogatory treatment of the work), and the *right of retraction*. It observed that, except for the dissemination right, these rights arise from the author's unique and privileged relationship to his own creation, and are properly termed "moral rights."
- It examined **Article 6bis of the Berne Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works**, which entitles an author, independently of and even after transfer of economic rights, to object to any distortion, mutilation, or other modification of, or derogatory action in relation to, his work that would be prejudicial to his honour or reputation. The court used this as the international benchmark against which Section 57 was framed and later amended.
- It traced the legislative history of Section 57: in its original 1957 form, the qualifying phrase "prejudicial to his honour or reputation" attached only to clause (b) (any other action in relation to the work), while clause (a) (distortion, mutilation, or modification) was not so qualified — making the original provision, in the court's assessment, wider than what the Berne Convention strictly required. The 1994 amendment (effective 10.5.1995) restructured the section so that the phrase "prejudicial to his honour or reputation" now qualifies the entire right to restrain or claim damages in respect of distortion, mutilation, modification, or other act, thereby bringing Indian law "at par" with the Berne Convention and making proof of prejudice to honour or reputation a *sine qua non* for the remedies of injunction and damages.
- The court then addressed the specific interpretive question of whether "mutilation" encompasses destruction. It recorded the two competing views: the narrower view (mutilation means partial alteration, and a wholly destroyed work, being non-existent, cannot cause reputational prejudice through its condition) versus the wider view (destruction is the "extreme and ultimate form of mutilation," rendering a work imperfect in the most complete sense, and reduces the volume of the author's creative corpus, thereby prejudicing his reputation).
- The court expressly adopted the **wider view**, holding that Section 57 should be interpreted to include destruction of a work of art as an extreme form of mutilation, since it diminishes the author's creative corpus and is therefore prejudicial to his honour and reputation.

**The cultural heritage dimension:** Building on this, the court reasoned that authorship is a matter of historical fact that identifies not only the creator but his contribution to national culture, and that the right of integrity, properly understood, ultimately contributes to the integrity of a nation's cultural domain. It held that the language of Section 57 does not exclude a right of integrity in relation to cultural heritage, and that where a work has attained the status of a nation's cultural heritage, this right extends to protecting the integrity of the work in that capacity.

**Reliance on scholarly and international materials:** In support of this reasoning, the court referred to:
- An article by Mira T. Sundara Rajan in the *International Journal of Cultural Property* (2001), cited for the proposition that moral rights of attribution and integrity serve not only individual authors but a public interest in preserving historical truth and cultural knowledge, and promote social respect for creativity.
- India's **10th Five Year Plan (2002–07)**, which articulates a vision of culture encompassing national identity and tangible/intangible heritage including sculpture and visual arts, and records the Government's own commitment to preservation of cultural heritage through successive plans.
- The **1970 UNESCO Convention** on prohibiting illicit import, export, and transfer of cultural property, which recognises States' moral obligation to respect cultural heritage and defines "cultural property" to include original works of statuary art and sculpture.
- The **1966 UNESCO Declaration of the Principles of International Cultural Co-operation**, one aim of which is to enable access to and enjoyment of the arts as part of cultural life.
- The **1972 UNESCO Convention Concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage**, which includes "works of monumental sculpture" within its definition of cultural heritage and obliges States to take measures for its protection and rehabilitation.
- The **1995 UNIDROIT Convention on Stolen or Illegally Exported Cultural Objects**.
- The **International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (1966)**, Article 15(1), recognising the right of authors to benefit from protection of the moral and material interests in their scientific, literary, or artistic productions.

From these instruments, the court distilled three obligations placed on States regarding cultural rights: to *respect*, to *protect*, and to *preserve* them.

**Use of precedent — *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan*:** The court cited ***Vishaka and Ors. v. State of Rajasthan and Ors.*** (1997) 6 SCC 241, in which the Supreme Court read international conventions not inconsistent with Indian law into the constitutional guarantee of gender equality, formulating guidelines in the absence of domestic law occupying the field. The court invoked this precedent as a methodological authority — for the *principle* that Indian courts may draw upon international conventions to interpret and give content to domestic rights where domestic law does not fully occupy the field — and applied the same interpretive method to give Section 57 a content informed by international cultural-heritage instruments, notwithstanding that India is a signatory to them.

**Application to facts:** The court held that the mural, as a result of the plaintiff's creative effort, had not only enhanced his personal celebrity but had itself attained the status of a modern national treasure. The undisputed evidence of destruction (from the reports of B.C. Sanyal, P.N. Mago, and Kapila Vatsyayan) meant that defendants' counsel did not even attempt to argue that the destruction and damage were in dispute.

**Ratio decidendi on Issues 2 & 3:** (i) Section 57 confers on the author moral rights of paternity and integrity that exist independently of, and survive, assignment of copyright. (ii) The term "mutilation" in Section 57 must be interpreted widely to include destruction of a work, as the most extreme form of mutilation, since destruction reduces the author's creative corpus and is thereby prejudicial to his honour and reputation. (iii) Where a work has attained the status of a nation's cultural heritage, the author's right of integrity under Section 57 extends to protecting the integrity of the work in relation to that cultural heritage.

**Finding:** Issues 2 and 3 were decided in favour of the plaintiff. The plaintiff was held entitled to maintain the action under Section 57 notwithstanding vesting of copyright in the defendants, and the defendants were held to have violated both the plaintiff's personal moral right of integrity and the integrity of the work in relation to the nation's cultural heritage.

---

### Issues 4 & 5: Damage and Relief

The court held that the mural, in whatever form it survived, was "too precious to be reduced to scrap and languish in the warehouse of the Government of India." It held that only the plaintiff had the right to recreate his own work, and consequently the right to receive the broken-down remnants. It further held that the plaintiff was entitled to compensation for loss of reputation, honour, and mental injury resulting from the defendants' conduct.

---

## 6. Decision

The suit was decreed in favour of the plaintiff and against the defendants, granting:

1. **Mandatory injunction** directing the defendants to return the remnants of the mural to the plaintiff within two weeks.
2. **Declaration** that all rights in the mural would henceforth vest in the plaintiff, with the defendants retaining no rights whatsoever in it.
3. **Declaration** that the plaintiff would have the absolute right to recreate the mural at any place and to sell it.
4. **Damages** of Rs. 5 lacs, carrying simple interest at 9% p.a. from the date of judgment if not paid within one month.
5. **Costs** in favour of the plaintiff.

## 7. Legal Significance

- **Wide construction of "mutilation" in Section 57:** The judgment holds that destruction of a work of art is the extreme form of mutilation and falls within the scope of Section 57, because it diminishes the volume of the author's creative corpus and is thereby prejudicial to his honour and reputation. This resolved, in the author's favour, a live interpretive tension between a narrow view (destruction cannot prejudice reputation because there is nothing left to view) and the wider view ultimately adopted by the court.
- **Moral rights independent of, and surviving, assignment of economic copyright:** The judgment reaffirms, consistently with Section 57's own text and Article 6bis of the Berne Convention, that moral rights of paternity and integrity subsist in the author independently of the economic copyright, even where that copyright has been wholly assigned — as it had been here, to the Government.
- **Linking moral rights to national cultural heritage:** The judgment extends the right of integrity beyond the author's personal reputation to encompass protection of a work's integrity in relation to the cultural heritage of the nation, once the work has attained the status of a national treasure — an interpretation the court found is not excluded by the language of Section 57 and is reinforced by India's obligations under various international cultural-heritage instruments.
- **Use of international instruments and comparative/scholarly material to inform domestic statutory interpretation:** The court's extensive reliance on the Berne Convention, UNESCO instruments, the UNIDROIT Convention, the ICESCR, and scholarly commentary — buttressed by the *Vishaka* methodology of reading international conventions into domestic legal guarantees — demonstrates a mode of purposive interpretation of Indian copyright law informed by India's international commitments.
- **Practical remedy fashioned to fit the moral-rights violation:** Rather than confining relief to damages or injunction, the court crafted a remedy — return of the remnants and vesting of full rights (including the right to recreate and sell the work) in the author — tailored to vindicate the moral right of integrity in a case of destruction, reflecting the judgment's broader reading of what redress under Section 57 can encompass.
