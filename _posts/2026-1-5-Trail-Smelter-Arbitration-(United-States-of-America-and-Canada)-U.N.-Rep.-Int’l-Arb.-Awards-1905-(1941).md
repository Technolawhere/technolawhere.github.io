# Trail Smelter Arbitration (United State/Canada), 3 U.N. Rep. Int’l Arb. Awards 1905 (1941)

## Facts:

Main issue was regarding a Canadian smelter, operating in Canada, near the Canada - US border, whose capacity had been increased, leading to significant increase in emissions of SO2 (sulphur dioxide). It was claimed by the US that these fumes travelled along the Columbia River Valley and caused damage within the State of Washington.

A tribunal was established to adjudicate the dispute between the two countries with primary focus on the following issues: 

## Issues: 

1. Whether damage had occurred in the State of Washington since January 1, 1932, and the appropriate indemnity.
   
3. Whether the Smelter should be required to refrain from causing future damage.
   
5. The specific measures or régime to be adopted by the Smelter.
   
7. What indemnity or compensation should be paid based on the decisions regarding the aforementioned questions.

## Observations 

### Regarding the mechanism of pollution

A pivotal part of the discussion involved how the fumes reached the United States. While both governments initially theorised that gas was carried by surface winds, the Tribunal rejected this theory. Instead, it adopted a more complex scientific explanation:

• Diurnal Fumigations: Occurring in late spring and summer, these are caused by the sun heating the earth's atmosphere, leading to a mixing of gas-carrying air with surface air.

• Winter Fumigations: These occur when gas-carrying air currents move at sufficient velocity to mix with the surface atmosphere, often at night when surface winds subside.

• Observation on Concentration: The Tribunal concluded that the concentration of sulphur dioxide drops rapidly up to 16 miles downstream from the Smelter (6 miles from the border), after which the decline becomes more gradual.

### When can the court take action in disputes like the present one?

Note: The tribunal noted that as there were no existing international cases specifically regarding air pollution, analogies from water pollution and United States Supreme Court decisions involving disputes between "quasi-sovereign" States could be used to address the issue before it. 

**Missouri v. Illinois (200 U.S. 496, 521)**: For court to intervene in a transborder dispute, case must be of serious magnitude and clearly and fully proved. 

The tribunal adopted this "serious magnitude" threshold to determine when an international injury warrants legal redress.

**New York v. New Jersey (256 U.S. 296, 309)**: In suits between States, the burden of proof is much higher than in ordinary private litigation; the invasion of rights must be established by clear and convincing evidence.

Applying this principle in the present case, the Tribunal held that this higher evidentiary standard applied to the "extraordinary power" granted to it under the Convention.

### Regarding the claims for indemnity

The analysis of the claims was done based on the evidence. Regarding Cleared Land (Crops), damage through reduced yield was confirmed for 1932–1936, but no proof was found for 1937. Regarding uncleared/Non-crop Land, the Tribunal rejected the U.S. method of using crop-yield ratios to estimate damage. Instead, it applied the American court measure of damages: the reduction in the value of the use or the rental value of the land. Claims for injury to livestock productivity (milk/wool) and urban property in Northport were dismissed due to a lack of clear proof. The claim for "reduced economic status" of residents leading to business loss was rejected as being too indirect, remote, and uncertain to be appraised.

The Tribunal awarded a final indemnity of $78,000 for damages up to October 1, 1937, with 6% annual interest until payment.

### Core concept 

"Under the principles of international law... no State has the right to use or permit the use of its territory in such a manner as to cause injury by fumes in or to the territory of another or the properties or persons therein, when the case is of serious consequence and the injury is established by clear and convincing evidence"

## Award

The Tribunal concluded that Canada is responsible in international law for the conduct of the Trail Smelter and has a continuous duty to ensure the Smelter conforms to these international obligations.
