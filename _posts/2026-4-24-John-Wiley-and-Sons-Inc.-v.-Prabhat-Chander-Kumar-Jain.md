# John Wiley & Sons Inc. & Ors. v. Prabhat Chander Kumar Jain & Ors.
### Delhi High Court | IA No. 11331/2008 in CS (OS) No. 1960/2008 | Decided on May 17, 2010 | Coram: Manmohan Singh, J.

---

## 1. One-Minute Summary

International academic publishers (Wiley, Cengage, Pearson) were selling **Low Price Editions (LPEs)** in India and neighbouring territories at steeply discounted rates compared to their US/international editions, with the books expressly marked as restricted for sale only in specified territories. The defendants, a Delhi-based bookseller operating through websites like alibris.com, purchased these LPEs in India and **exported/sold them online to customers in the US, UK and other countries outside the permitted territories**, marketing them as being "identical" to the US edition at a fraction of the price.

The core legal question was whether **exporting copies purchased from an exclusive licensee, in breach of territorial restrictions imposed by the copyright owner, amounts to infringement of copyright** under the Indian Copyright Act, 1957, especially since "export" is not expressly listed as an infringing act under Section 51, unlike "import" under Section 51(b)(iv).

The Court held that:
- The **rights of the "owner" of copyright and the rights of an "exclusive licensee" are distinct** for the purposes of Section 51 infringement analysis. Under Section 54(a), an exclusive licensee is treated as an "owner" only for the limited purposes of Chapter XII (civil remedies), not for defining the substantive scope of rights under Sections 14 and 51.
- The **owner's right to issue copies "not already in circulation"** under Section 14(1)(a)(ii) is **not territorially limited**, whereas the licensee's rights are limited to the territory permitted under the license.
- A purchaser (here, the defendant) who buys books from a licensee **with notice** of the territorial restriction (printed on the book itself) cannot acquire better rights than the licensee possessed, and by selling into unauthorised territories, violates the **owner's** right to control circulation there. This is a **primary infringement**, distinct from and independent of the import-based **secondary infringement** under Section 51(b)(iv).
- On the **first sale/exhaustion doctrine**, since the "first sale" in this case was made by the **exclusive licensees** (who had only limited, territorially restricted rights) and not by the **owners**, the doctrine, even if applicable, would exhaust only the licensee's rights, not the owner's broader worldwide rights. The owner's right to complain about unauthorised extra-territorial circulation therefore survives.
- The Court further observed, following its own precedent in *Warner Bros. Entertainment Inc. v. Santosh V.G.*, that in the absence of an express statutory provision for **international exhaustion** (unlike the US), Indian law is better understood as following a **regional exhaustion** principle.

**Decision:** The Court confirmed the ex-parte ad-interim injunction, allowed the plaintiffs' injunction application (IA No. 11331/2008), and dismissed the defendants' application for vacation of the injunction (IA No. 14897/2008), restraining the defendants from advertising, offering for sale, or exporting the plaintiffs' publications to territories outside those specified on the books.

## 3. Facts and Background

- **The Plaintiffs:** Six plaintiffs comprising three pairs of owner–licensee relationships:
  - John Wiley & Sons Inc. (P1, owner) and Wiley India Pvt. Ltd. (P2, exclusive licensee).
  - Cengage Learning Inc. (P3, owner) and Cengage Learning India Pvt. Ltd. (P4, exclusive licensee).
  - Pearson Education Inc. (P5, owner) and Dorling Kindersley India Pvt. Ltd. (P6, exclusive licensee).
- The plaintiffs published **"Low Price Editions"/"Wiley Student Editions"** of their academic books for sale only in India and specified neighbouring/Asian territories (Bangladesh, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Indonesia, Philippines, Vietnam, Bhutan, Maldives, etc.), at prices far below the international/US editions. The books carried explicit printed notices restricting sale to these territories and bore a "Low Price Edition"/"LPE" band.
- **The Defendants:** Defendant No. 3, Technischer Overseas Pvt. Ltd., a Delhi-based bookseller, operated online through websites such as **www.alibris.com**, **www.biblio.com**, and **www.biblion.co.uk**, offering these LPEs for worldwide sale, describing them to customers as **"identical, word for word"** to the American edition, differing only in ISBN, at a fraction of the US price. Defendant No. 3 was allegedly owned/operated by Defendant Nos. 1 and 2.
- **Test Purchases:** In July 2008, the plaintiffs' representative in New York ordered two titles ("Microwave Engineering" and "Fundamentals of Fluid Mechanics") priced at roughly US $16–17 (versus US $149–173 for the US editions) and received Wiley Student Editions restricted for sale in India and neighbouring countries. In August 2008, a further order of five titles was placed and similarly fulfilled with restricted editions, delivered by post/courier using allegedly false sender details.
- **Prior US Proceedings:** A consent decree dated 4 February 2008 had already been passed against Defendant No. 2 (along with associates) by the US District Court, Southern District of New York, permanently enjoining them from infringing the plaintiffs' registered copyrights/trademarks, including through sale to US residents of books marked to prohibit US resale.
- **Procedural History:** On 17.09.2008, the Delhi High Court granted an ex-parte ad-interim injunction restraining the defendants from advertising, offering for sale, or exporting the plaintiffs' publications outside the specified territories. The defendants appeared, filed a written statement, and moved IA No. 14897/2008 under Order XXXIX Rule 4 CPC for vacation/modification of that order. Both applications were heard together and decided by this judgment.

---

## 4. Legal Questions Before the Court

The Court organised its discussion under the following heads (para 44):

1. **Whether the defendants' acts of exporting/selling the territorially-restricted Low Price Editions outside the permitted territories amount to infringement of copyright** under the Copyright Act, 1957, notwithstanding that "export" (unlike "import") is not expressly listed as an infringing act under Section 51?
2. **Whether the doctrine of first sale/exhaustion of rights applies**, and if so, what is its impact on the plaintiffs' claim, and particularly whether the first sale made by the exclusive licensees to the defendants exhausts the rights of the owners of copyright as well?
3. (Incidental) Whether the Delhi High Court has territorial jurisdiction to entertain the suit?

---

## 5. Discussion on Each Legal Question

### 5.1 Question One: Does Exporting in Breach of Territorial Restrictions Amount to Infringement?

**Arguments of the Plaintiffs (Mr. Mukul Rohatgi, Sr. Adv.):**
- Section 14 confers on the owner the exclusive right "to issue copies of the work to the public not being copies already in circulation." Diverting LPEs meant for India to other countries violates this right because those copies are not "already in circulation" in the destination country.
- Section 51 deems infringement to occur where any person does an act without licence or in contravention of licence conditions — the defendants' conduct fell squarely within this.
- Reliance was placed on the Division Bench decision in **Penguin Books Ltd. v. India Book Distributors**, 26 (1984) DLT 316 (DB), where import of Britain-published books from the USA without licence from the exclusive licensee was held to infringe copyright; the Court there observed that copyright law is territorial and that sale within one country cannot abrogate the rights of an exclusive licensee in another country. The plaintiffs argued the same logic applies equally to exports as to imports.
- The words "already in circulation" in Section 14 must be read meaningfully — a copy in circulation in India is not "in circulation" in the USA/UK.
- Reliance was placed on several US decisions (*Quality King Distributors, Inc. v. L'Anza Research International, Inc.*; *Omega SA v. Costco Wholesale Corp.*; *Microsoft Corp. v. Big Boy Distribution LLC*; *Pearson Education Inc. v. Jun Liao*) to argue that the first sale doctrine in the US itself does not extend to protect resale of copies manufactured/first sold abroad, and is therefore inapplicable here.

**Arguments of the Defendants (Mr. Rahul Gupta):**
- Neither Section 51 nor Section 14 recognises "export" as an infringing act; only "import" of infringing copies is expressly covered under Section 51(b)(iv) read with Section 2(m). Since the Legislature expressly included import but not export, export cannot be read into the infringement provisions.
- The books were **lawfully purchased in India**; once purchased, the defendants' rights over them (including the right to resell) crystallised, and there is no infringing act within India. At best, any infringement would occur, if at all, in the country of import.
- The first sale/exhaustion doctrine applies: once the first sale is effected in India, the copyright owner exhausts the right to control subsequent sales (relying on *Bobbs-Merrill Co. v. Straus* (US, 1908), *Quality King* (US, 1998), and the Delhi High Court's own decision in ***Warner Bros. Entertainment Inc. v. Santosh V.G.***, MIPR 2009(2) 175.)
- No privity of contract existed between the plaintiffs and the defendants, so the territorial restriction printed on the books could not bind the defendants.
- The suit disclosed no cause of action (Order VII Rule 11 CPC); the relief sought was anti-competitive and raised trade barriers; and the plaintiffs' own distributors were themselves exporting books, making the plaintiffs' conduct inequitable.

**The Court's Reasoning and Analysis:**

The Court began by undertaking a structural analysis of Sections 13, 14 and 51 of the Act:

- **Distinguishing "owner" from "exclusive licensee":** The Court drew a sharp distinction between the rights of the *owner* of copyright and those of an *exclusive licensee*. It noted that Section 54(a) defines "owner of copyright" to *include* an exclusive licensee, but only **"for the purposes of this Chapter"**, i.e., Chapter XII, dealing with civil remedies (including Section 55). This, the Court held, was a **deliberate and limited legislative extension**, meant only to give the exclusive licensee an independent right to sue for infringement; it does **not** mean that for the purposes of Sections 14 and 51 (which lie outside Chapter XII), the exclusive licensee is to be equated with the owner. The Court invoked the literal rule of interpretation (citing *Association for Development v. Union of India*, 167 (2010) DLT 481 (DB)) to hold that where the statutory language is plain, courts cannot read in words that are not there.
- **Consequence of this distinction:** Since Section 51 speaks of infringement of the rights **"conferred... upon the owner of the copyright,"** the Court held that infringement must be measured against the rights of the owner (Plaintiff Nos. 1, 3, 5), not the more limited, territorially-fettered rights of the exclusive licensees (Plaintiff Nos. 2, 4, 6).
- **Owner's rights are not territorially limited:** The Court held that the owner's right under Section 14(1)(a)(ii), to issue copies of the work not already in circulation, is a **worldwide right**, not confined by any territorial limitation, unless the owner itself chooses to fetter it by licence. This was reinforced by Section 19(6), which provides that if the territorial extent of an assignment/licence is not specified, it is *deemed* to be limited to India, a statutory recognition, in the Court's view, that absent such deeming, an owner's rights (and the ability to license them) extend worldwide.
- **Licensee's rights are territorially fettered:** By contrast, the rights of the exclusive licensees (P2, P4, P6) are subject to whatever territorial restrictions the owner chose to impose by the licensing agreement. In the present case, the licensees were restricted to India and the specified neighbouring countries.
- **Position of a purchaser from a restricted licensee:** Relying on **Copinger and Skone James on Copyright** (14th Edn., para 5-193, "Position of the Licensee"), the Court noted the settled equitable principle that a licence granted by a copyright owner binds every successor in title to the licensee's interest, **except a bona fide purchaser for value without notice** of the licence/restriction. Since the territorial restriction was **printed on the very books** purchased by the defendants, and the defendants themselves advertised the books as "international editions" distinct from the US edition (evincing actual knowledge of the restriction), the defendants could not claim to be purchasers without notice. A purchaser with notice cannot obtain rights greater than those possessed by the licensee from whom he purchased. The Court drew an analogy to licensed computer software, which is similarly sold subject to conditions binding on downstream purchasers with notice.
- **Rejecting the "export is not listed" argument:** The Court held that the real question was not whether "export" per se is recognised as an infringing act, but whether the **owner's distribution right** to control circulation of copies not yet in circulation in a given territory can be circumvented by a person who purchases from a territorially-restricted licensee and then sells into unauthorised territories. If such conduct violates the owner's right under Section 14(1)(a)(ii) read with Section 51(a), it is infringement regardless of whether it is labelled "export." The Court expressly held this to be a **primary infringement**, distinct from and independent of the secondary infringement contemplated for importation under Section 51(b)(iv); the two forms of infringement, the Court noted, "can coexist simultaneously": the exporter may commit primary infringement in India while the importer in the destination country may separately commit secondary infringement there under that country's law.
- **Disjunctive grounds under Section 51(a):** The Court also noted that Section 51(a) recognises infringement in the alternative — either (i) acting without any licence at all, or (ii) acting in contravention of licence conditions. The defendants' conduct was held to fall under both limbs: it was an unlicensed act vis-à-vis the owners, and simultaneously a violation of the territorial conditions of the licence granted to Plaintiff Nos. 2, 4, 6.

The Court finally held that the defendants' acts of purchasing territorially-restricted Low Price Editions from the exclusive licensees (with notice of the restriction) and selling/exporting them to unauthorised territories were **prima facie acts of primary infringement of copyright** under Section 51(a) of the Copyright Act, 1957, violating the rights of the owners (P1, P3, P5) as well as contravening the conditions of the licence granted to P2, P4, P6.

### 5.2 Question Two: Applicability and Impact of the First Sale/Exhaustion Doctrine

**Arguments of the Defendants:** The Explanation to Section 14 ("a copy which has been sold once shall be deemed to be a copy already in circulation") statutorily embeds the first sale doctrine, which states that once the copyright owner exercises the right to issue copies, it loses all further right to control subsequent sales of that copy, in the copyright law. Reliance was placed on *Bobbs-Merrill Co. v. Straus* (US, 1908), *Quality King Distributors v. L'Anza Research International* (US, 1998), and most significantly on the Delhi High Court's own decision in **Warner Bros. Entertainment Inc. v. Santosh V.G.**, MIPR 2009(2) 175, which recognised the doctrine of exhaustion of rights in relation to literary works under the Indian Act.

**The Court's Reasoning:**

- The Court closely analysed its own earlier reasoning in ***Warner Bros. v. Santosh V.G.***:
  - In para 58 of *Warner Bros.*, exhaustion of rights was described as linked to the distribution right: once copies are released with the owner's consent, the owner's control over further use of *those specific copies* is limited.
  - In para 63, *Warner Bros.* had held that the Explanation to Section 14 ("a copy which has been sold once shall be deemed to be a copy already in circulation") is confined **only to literary, dramatic, and musical works** under Section 14(a), and does **not** extend to cinematograph films, computer programmes, or other classes of work listed separately under Section 14(b)–(f). Since *Warner Bros.* itself concerned cinematograph films, the Court there ultimately held the exhaustion principle to be **inapplicable** to the facts before it, even though it recognised the principle exists for literary works.
  - In para 65, *Warner Bros.* had also flagged the potentially "catastrophic commercial consequences" of extending exhaustion to licensees, e.g., a distributor licensed to exhibit/rent a film only in one territory could, on the defendants' logic, exploit it in other territories where it was never released, defeating the owner's right to commercially partition markets via licensing.
  - *Warner Bros.* (para 59) also noted that whether exhaustion is **international** or **regional** depends on the statutory scheme of the country in question: the US, by express statutory design, follows international exhaustion, whereas the UK/EU follow regional exhaustion (limited to the EU market).
- Applying this in the present case, the Court held: since **India's Copyright Act contains no express provision analogous to the US position** mandating international exhaustion, it would be "appropriate to confine the applicability of the same to regional exhaustion."
- **Crucially, however**, the Court held that this question did not even need to be conclusively decided on the facts of this case, because of a separate and dispositive reason: the **"first sale" here was effected by the exclusive licensees (P2, P4, P6), not by the owners (P1, P3, P5)**. The licensees' own rights were, by definition, limited and territorially fettered by the licence agreements. Therefore, even if the first sale doctrine is applied, it can at most **exhaust the rights of the licensees** (to the extent those rights existed). It **cannot exhaust the broader, unfettered rights of the owners**, who never sold the books themselves in the first place. The owner's right to complain of continuing infringement in unauthorised territories, and of breach of the licence conditions, therefore survives untouched.
- The Court rejected the analogy to the software/licensing principle sought by the defendants, holding that accepting the defendants' argument would produce the same "absurd results" flagged in *Warner Bros.* para 65 that a licensee (or purchaser from a licensee) could, by mere fact of purchase, override all territorial restrictions the owner had carefully imposed to partition its markets.

Thus, the first sale/exhaustion doctrine, even where applicable to literary works in India (as opposed to cinematograph films), would in this case only exhaust the rights of the exclusive licensees who made the first sale and not the rights of the owners of copyright, whose right to complain of infringement in unauthorised territories remained intact.

---
### 5.3 Question 3: Ancillary question on jurisdiction

The Court held that under Section 20 CPC, Delhi courts had jurisdiction since the defendants carried on business there, orders were taken and executed from Delhi, and profits from the sales were realised in Delhi. This was sufficient to find jurisdiction under both Section 20(a) and 20(c) CPC, without any question of extra-territorial application of the Act.

## 6. Decision of the Court

The Court held that the plaintiffs had made out a **prima facie case** of infringement of copyright, that the **balance of convenience** lay in favour of the plaintiffs (who would suffer greater inconvenience if the injunction were not confirmed, especially given the defendants had already given an undertaking before a US court against similar infringing acts), and that the plaintiffs would suffer **irreparable injury** absent an injunction (applied the settled parameters for grant of injunction laid down in ***Dalpat Kumar v. Prahlad Singh***, AIR 1993 SC 276., i.e., prima facie case, balance of convenience and irreparable loss or injury)

Accordingly:
- **IA No. 11331/2008** (plaintiffs' application for temporary injunction) was **allowed**.
- **IA No. 14897/2008** (defendants' application for vacation of the ex-parte injunction) was **dismissed**.
- The defendants, their agents, assigns, and sister concerns were restrained, till disposal of the suit, from advertising, offering for sale, or exporting any publications of the plaintiffs to countries outside the territories specified on the books.

The Court clarified that all observations were prima facie/tentative in nature and would have no bearing on the final merits of the suit.

## 7. Main Legal Implications for Copyright Law


1. Owner vs. Exclusive Licensee distinction and the consequent exhaution application of exhaution to the rights of licensee while the rights of the owner remains unaffected.
2. Owner's distribution right is not inherently territorially confined
3. Notice defeats the "innocent purchaser" defence: A purchaser who buys territorially-restricted copies from a licensee, with notice of the restriction (e.g., printed on the book), cannot claim rights greater than the licensee possessed. 
4. India follows (or leans toward) regional, not international, exhaustion: Building on its earlier reasoning in *Warner Bros. v. Santosh V.G.*, the Court held that in the absence of an express statutory provision for international exhaustion (as exists in the US), the Indian Copyright Act is better read as embodying a regional exhaustion principle.
5. "Export" need not be expressly named in Section 51 to constitute infringement: *Substance* of the owner's right and control over first circulation of copies in a given territory, governs the infringement analysis, not the *label* attached to the defendant's conduct. Conduct that undermines the owner's territorial licensing structure can constitute *primary infringement* even though "export" is not separately listed as an infringing act (unlike "import" under Section 51(b)(iv)), and such primary infringement can coexist with, and is analytically distinct from, secondary infringement that may separately arise upon importation in the destination country.
