# Eastern Book Company & Ors. v. D.B. Modak & Anr. AIR 2008 SC 809

**Court:** Supreme Court of India

**Bench:** B.N. Agrawal and P.P. Naolekar, JJ.

**Author:** P.P. Naolekar, J.

**Date of Judgment:** 12 December 2007

**Case No.:** Civil Appeal No. 6472 of 2004 (with Civil Appeal No. 6905 of 2004 and Contempt Petition (Civil) No. 158 of 2006)

---

## 1. In a minute

Eastern Book Company (EBC), publisher of the law report *Supreme Court Cases* (SCC), sued two CD-ROM legal database publishers: Spectrum Business Support Ltd. (whose product was "Grand Jurix") and Regent Data Tech Pvt. Ltd. (whose product was "The Laws"), alleging that they had copied verbatim the copy-edited text of Supreme Court judgments as it appeared in SCC, along with EBC's sequencing, selection, arrangement, paragraph numbering, footnote numbers and cross-references. EBC claimed that its copy-edited version of the judgments, created by inserting cross-citations, corrections, headnotes, paragraph numbering and other editorial inputs into the raw judgment text obtained from the Registry of the Supreme Court, was itself an "original literary work" under Section 13 of the Copyright Act, 1957, entitled to copyright protection as a whole.

The central question before the Court was: what standard of **originality** must a **derivative work** (a work built on pre-existing public-domain material, here the raw text of judgments) satisfy in order to attract copyright protection, and did EBC's copy-edited version of the judgments, taken as a whole, meet that standard?

The Supreme Court surveyed the English "sweat of the brow" line of authorities, the American "minimal creativity" standard from *Feist Publications*, and the Canadian Supreme Court's intermediate "skill and judgment" standard from *CCH Canadian Ltd. v. Law Society of Upper Canada*. It expressly adopted the Canadian standard: a derivative work need not be "creative" in the sense of being novel or non-obvious but must originate from the author (not be copied), and must be the product of the author's exercise of skill and judgment that is not so trivial as to be a purely mechanical exercise.

Applying this standard, the Court held that the bulk of EBC's editorial inputs like cross-citations, corrected case names, page/paragraph references to quotations, margin headings, removal of abbreviations, punctuation corrections, and similar formatting changes were the product of labour, skill and capital, but not of the requisite degree of creative or judgmental input, and therefore did **not** generate a fresh copyright in the copy-edited judgment text as a whole. However, the Court carved out two specific categories of inputs: (i) the segregation of the running text of a judgment into separate numbered paragraphs together with internal cross-paragraph referencing, and (ii) the insertion of editorial characterisations of judicial opinions such as "concurring," "partly dissenting," "partly concurring," etc. These, according to the Court, involved genuine legal understanding and judgment, and therefore protectable by copyright in favour of EBC.

The appeals were **partly allowed**. 

---

## 2. Facts and Background of the Case

- Eastern Book Company (Appellant No. 1) is a partnership firm, and EBC Publishing Pvt. Ltd. (Appellant No. 2) a company, both engaged in publishing law books, including the well-known law report *Supreme Court Cases* (SCC), continuously published since 1969.
- EBC procures certified copies of judgments, orders and records of proceedings from the Registrar of the Supreme Court. This raw text is then copy-edited by a team of assistant staff, who add cross-references, standardise and format the text, insert paragraph numbering, verify quotations, and add other inputs to make the judgments "user friendly." Headnotes, comprising a short catch/lead-word note, and a long note summarising facts and extracting relevant portions of the judgment are separately prepared by Appellant No. 3, Surendra Malik.
- EBC claimed that this copy-editing process required considerable skill, labour, expertise and capital expenditure, and that the resulting copy-edited version of the judgments constituted an original literary work under Section 13 of the Copyright Act, 1957, in which EBC alone held copyright under Section 14. EBC did **not** claim copyright in the raw text of the judgments themselves, nor any monopoly over publishing Supreme Court judgments as such.
- Respondent Spectrum Business Support Ltd. published a CD-ROM software titled "Grand Jurix" (subject of Civil Appeal No. 6472/2004), and Respondent Regent Data Tech Pvt. Ltd. published a CD-ROM titled "The Laws" (subject of Civil Appeal No. 6905/2004). EBC alleged that these products had lifted, verbatim, EBC's sequencing, selection and arrangement of cases, together with the entire copy-edited text.
- EBC sought temporary injunctions in Suit Nos. 758/2000 and 624/2000 before the Delhi High Court. A learned Single Judge, by common judgment dated 17.1.2001, dismissed EBC's applications for interim injunction and vacated the earlier stay. Before the Single Judge, the respondents conceded that EBC held copyright in its headnotes and undertook not to copy them.
- On appeal, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court (order dated 9.3.2001) permitted the respondents, pending disposal of the appeals, to sell their CD-ROMs with the text of Supreme Court judgments together with their own headnotes (not copied from EBC's).
- On final hearing, the Division Bench held that EBC was not the "author" of Supreme Court judgments; that merely making corrections, inserting paragraph numbers, or arranging judgments in a particular manner did not make the copy-edited judgments an original literary work of EBC; that reproduction of judgments falls within the exception in Section 52(1)(q) of the Act and is therefore not infringement; but that EBC's headnotes (since they involve selecting and collecting sentences from various parts of a judgment) and its footnotes/editorial notes (being the publisher's own creation) did attract copyright. The Division Bench accordingly modified the Single Judge's order, permitting the respondents to sell CD-ROMs with the judgment text and their own headnotes/editorial notes, but restraining them from copying EBC's headnotes, footnotes and editorial notes.
- EBC appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave.

---

## 3. Legal Questions Before the Court

The Supreme Court framed the questions for determination as follows (para 10):

1. **What shall be the standard of originality** in the copy-edited judgments of the Supreme Court, which constitute a derivative work, and what is required in a derivative work for it to be treated as the original work of an author, thereby attracting copyright protection under the Copyright Act, 1957?
2. **Whether the entire version of the copy-edited text of the judgments** published in EBC's law report SCC would be entitled to copyright as an original literary work as a whole (the copy-edited judgments being claimed as an inextricable and inseparable admixture of the copy-editing inputs and the raw text) **or** whether EBC would instead be entitled to copyright only in certain specific inputs put into the raw text?

---

## 4. Discussion on the Legal Questions

### 4.1 Arguments of the Parties

**Arguments for the Appellants (EBC):**
- Section 52(1)(q)(iv) of the Act does not bar recognition of copyright in the copy-edited version of judgments published in law reports; it merely exempts the reproduction/publication of the judgment itself (which is "government work" under Section 2(k), with the Government as first owner under Section 17(d)) from being an infringement. It does not permit a third party to copy *EBC's own version* created by the expenditure of independent skill, labour and capital.
- Copyright subsists in the copy-edited text because it is a derivative literary work created from pre-existing public-domain material (the judgments), and the exercise of independent skill, labour and capital in its creation entitles EBC to copyright. Literary merit is not required. Only requirement is that the skill, labour and capital employed should not be trivial or negligible.
- In assessing copyrightability, the work must be considered **as a whole**, and it is incorrect to dissect it into fragments and evaluate each fragment's copyrightability individually.
- A party wishing to reproduce or publish public-domain material is obliged to go back to the public-domain/common source, rather than misappropriating the effort of a party who has independently created its own version of that material.
- Reliance was placed on a long line of English, Indian and Commonwealth authorities discussed below (*Ladbroke*, *Walter v. Lane*, *Designers Guild*, *University of London Press*, *Kelly v. Morris*, *Parry v. Moring and Gollancz*, *Gopal Das v. Jagannath Prasad*, *V. Govindan v. E.M. Gopalakrishna Kone*, *C. Cunniah & Co. v. Balraj & Co.*, *Agarwala Publishing House*, *Gangavishnu Shrikisondas v. Moreshvar Bapuji Hegishte*, *Rai Toys Industries v. Munir Printing Press*, and *Macmillan v. Suresh Chandra Deb*).

**Arguments for the Respondents:**

*For Spectrum (per Mr. P.N. Lekhi):*
- A Supreme Court judgment is "government work" under Section 2(k)(iii), and by virtue of Section 17(d) the Government is the first owner of copyright therein in the absence of an agreement to the contrary.
- Section 52(1)(q)(iv) provides that publication of a judgment, unless prohibited, is not an infringement and hence the respondents' publication cannot amount to infringement of EBC's claimed copyright.
- EBC's SCC version is "merely a derivative work" lacking originality, as it does not depict independent creation or even a modicum of creativity; filling in names of parties or citations (both well-known and unchangeable parts of the idea) is not original work and cannot generate copyright, singly or in combination.

*For Regent Data Tech (per Ms. Pratibha M. Singh):*
- SCC, unlike a traditional English "law report" (where the reporter records arguments and facts in his own words), is really a "law journal" that reproduces judgments verbatim along with certain inputs.
- Since judgments are placed in the exception under Section 52(1)(q) in the public interest, and the derivative work claimed here (the copy-edited version) requires originality (meaning independent creation plus **at least some minimal degree of creativity**) the copy-editing inputs, being no more than "discoveries" of facts already in existence (as opposed to creation of new facts), cannot meet this test.
- The English decisions relied upon by EBC arose under the old UK Copyright Act of 1842, which did not contain the word "original" at all, and are therefore inapplicable to a statute (the Indian Act of 1957) that expressly requires "originality."
- Copy-editing inputs are discoveries/facts that can be expressed only in limited ways, and no copyright can subsist in such limited or unique modes of expression.
- Recognising copyright in the copy-edited version would effectively give EBC a monopoly over Supreme Court judgments, defeating the purpose of Section 52(1)(q)(iv), which is to keep judgments in the public domain.
- The applicable test should be the standard evolved by the United States Supreme Court for derivative works — the author must show the work resulted from an exercise of skill and judgment that is not so trivial as to be characterised as a purely mechanical exercise, and must possess at least some minimal degree of creativity (relying on *Feist Publications*).

### 4.2 Development of the Court's Reasoning

**4.2.1 Precedents considered by the Court**

The Court undertook an extensive survey of English, Indian, Canadian and American authorities:

- **Ladbroke (Football) Ltd. v. William Hill (Football) Ltd.**, [1964] 1 WLR 273 (HL) — concerned copying of football betting coupons. The House of Lords held that "original" does not require original or inventive thought, only that the work must not be copied but must originate from the author; and that, in judging whether a compilation is original, it is wrong to dissect it into fragments — the correct approach is first to ask whether the work *as a whole* is original, and only then whether the part taken is substantial. This case supported EBC's "whole work" argument but was ultimately not the touchstone the Court finally applied.
- **Walter v. Lane**, [1900] AC 539 (HL) — a *Times* reporter's verbatim shorthand transcription of Lord Rosebery's public speeches was held to attract copyright in the reporter's own report, even though the words themselves were not his; copyright has nothing to do with originality of ideas or literary merit, but rests on the principle that one should not avail oneself of another's skill, labour and expense by copying the written product.
- **Designers Guild Ltd. v. Russell Williams (Textiles) Ltd.**, [2000] 1 WLR 2416 (HL) — a fabric-design infringement case reaffirming that the law of copyright rests on the principle that a person who creates an original work by his own skill and labour enjoys an exclusive right to copy it.
- **University of London Press Ltd. v. University Tutorial Press Ltd.**, [1916] 2 Ch 601 — described by the Court as perhaps the most cited case on originality; examination papers were held to be literary work, and "original" was held to mean only that the work is not copied from another but originates from the author — novelty of form is not required.
- **Kelly v. Morris**, (1866) LR 1 Eq 697 — a directory case establishing the "industrious collection" principle: a subsequent compiler working from common sources of information must independently verify and gather the material himself (e.g., counting milestones afresh for a road-book), using a prior publication only to verify his own results, and not to save himself the labour of independent inquiry.
- **Parry v. Moring and Gollancz**, Cop Cas (1901-1904) 49 — the plaintiff's chronological arrangement and modern-English translation of historical letters was held to attract copyright in the editorial work, distinct from copyright in the underlying text; a rival editor could not simply lift the plaintiff's edited version.
- **Gopal Das v. Jagannath Prasad**, AIR 1938 All. 266 — compiling a book with considerable labour from various common sources, and digesting and arranging that material, attracts copyright; a defendant who instead lifts material from the plaintiff's compilation (rather than going to the common sources himself) infringes.
- **V. Govindan v. E.M. Gopalakrishna Kone**, AIR 1955 Madras 391 — an English-Tamil dictionary case where slavish, word-for-word copying (including errors) was found; the Court held that where a common source is relied upon as a defence, the defendant must prove he actually went to that common source using his own skill, labour and brain, and did not merely copy the plaintiff's work.
- **C. Cunniah & Co. v. Balraj & Co.**, AIR 1961 Madras 111 — concerning a devotional picture; the Court held that copyright does not require the subject-matter to be original or the idea novel — what is required is the expenditure of original skill or labour in *execution*.
- **Agarwala Publishing House v. Board of High School and Intermediate Education**, AIR 1967 All. 91 — examination-question-paper case holding that "original" under Section 13 does not imply originality of ideas, only that the work should originate from the author as a product of his labour and skill, rather than being copied.
- **Gangavishnu Shrikisondas v. Moreshvar Bapuji Hegishte**, ILR 13 Bom 358 — an annotated edition of an old Sanskrit religious text (*Vrtraj*), prepared with the assistance of pandits who re-cast, re-arranged and added footnotes to the text, was held to be entitled to copyright in the additions and re-arrangement, even though the underlying text was in the public domain.
- **Rai Toys Industries v. Munir Printing Press**, 1982 PTC 85 — preparation of Tambola ticket books, requiring skill and labour in arranging numbers into tables, was held to satisfy the test of original literary work.
- **Macmillan and Another v. Suresh Chandra Deb**, ILR 17 Cal 952 — a selection of songs and poems compiled into an anthology was held copyrightable because the selection required extensive reading, careful study, comparison, and the exercise of taste and judgment; a rival who simply adopted another's selection (even in a different arrangement) infringed.
- **Macmillan and Company v. K. and J. Cooper**, 1924 Privy Council 75 — concerning a compilation of unaltered, unmodified extracts from Sir Thomas North's translation of Plutarch's *Life of Alexander*; the Privy Council held that where selected passages are merely reprinted in their original form without being condensed, expanded, modified or reshaped, no copyright arises — the labour, skill and capital expended must impart to the product "some quality or character which the raw material did not possess" for copyright to attach.
- **CCH Canadian Ltd. v. Law Society of Upper Canada**, 2004 (1) SCR 339 (Canada) — arising from a photocopying dispute at the Great Library of the Law Society of Upper Canada, the Supreme Court of Canada surveyed the competing "sweat of the brow" and "creativity" standards and rejected both: sweat of the brow sets too low a bar (protecting labour and capital alone, without regard to authorship), while a pure creativity standard sets too high a bar (importing patent-law style novelty/non-obviousness). The Canadian Court instead adopted an intermediate "**skill and judgment**" standard — a work is original if it originates from the author (is not copied), and is the product of the author's exercise of skill and judgment that is not so trivial as to be characterised as a purely mechanical exercise. **This is the standard the Supreme Court of India expressly adopted** for the purposes of the present case (para 38).
- **Feist Publications Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co. Inc.**, 18 USPQ 2d 1275 (US Supreme Court) — concerning copying of white-pages telephone-directory listings, the US Supreme Court rejected the "sweat of the brow" doctrine and held that originality requires independent creation plus **at least some minimal degree of creativity**; facts are not copyrightable since they are discovered, not created, but a compiler's *selection and arrangement* of facts may be original if it entails a minimal degree of creativity; the primary objective of copyright is to promote the progress of science and useful arts, not merely to reward an author's labour.
- **Matthew Bender & Co., Inc. v. West Publishing Co.**, 158 F.3d 674 (2nd Cir. 1998) — directly on point, concerning West Publishing's additions of headnotes, syllabi, key numbers, parallel citations, attorney information and procedural-history data to judicial opinions. The US Court of Appeals held that such alterations — additions/arrangement of facts, or rearrangement of data already in the opinions — could be assessed for creativity in "selection and arrangement," and that West's choices were obvious, typical and lacking even minimal creativity. The Court identified relevant factors for assessing creativity in selection and arrangement: (i) the total number of options available, (ii) external factors limiting the viability of certain options, and (iii) prior uses rendering certain choices "garden variety."
- **Key Publications, Inc. v. Chinatown Today Publishing Enterprises, Inc.**, 945 F.2d 509 — a business-directory case setting out three requirements for a compilation to qualify for copyright: (1) collection and assembly of pre-existing data; (2) selection, coordination or arrangement of that data; and (3) the resulting work being original by virtue of that selection, coordination or arrangement.

**4.2.2 No precedent was expressly overruled.** The Court did not overturn any of the above decisions; rather, it treated the older English "sweat of the brow" line (*Walter v. Lane*, *University of London Press*, *Ladbroke*) and the pure "minimal creativity" American standard (*Feist*) as representing two ends of a spectrum, and consciously chose to follow the intermediate Canadian standard from *CCH Canadian* as most consistent with the scheme of the Indian Copyright Act, 1957 — noting that the reliance placed by the respondents on the argument that the older English decisions were inapplicable (having been decided under the pre-1911 UK statute, which lacked the word "original") did not need to be resolved definitively, since the Court in any case preferred the Canadian formulation.

**4.2.3 Use of statutory provisions and secondary sources.** The Court grounded its reasoning in the statutory text — Sections 2(k) (government work), 2(o) (literary work), 2(y) (work), 13(1) (subsistence of copyright), 14(1)(a) (meaning of copyright), 17 and its proviso (d) (Government as first owner of government work), and 52(1)(q)(iv) (exception for reproduction/publication of judgments) — to establish that Supreme Court judgments are "government work," that the Government (absent contrary agreement) is the first owner of copyright therein, and that reproduction or publication of such judgments is expressly excluded from infringement unless prohibited by the court concerned. This statutory foundation underpinned the Court's conclusion that the judgments themselves remain in the public domain, and that any copyright claimed by EBC could only attach to what EBC itself had originated over and above the raw judgment text.

**4.2.4 Acceptance and rejection of the parties' contentions.** The Court accepted the respondents' core contention that mere labour, skill and capital invested in copy-editing — without more — cannot generate copyright in a derivative work built on public-domain material; "sweat of the brow" alone was rejected as too low a standard. However, the Court did not go as far as the respondents' extreme submission (drawing on *Feist*) that only "minimal creativity" akin to the American standard would suffice, nor did it require novelty or non-obviousness akin to patent law. Instead, applying the Canadian "skill and judgment" formulation, the Court held that EBC's contentions succeeded only in part: most of its editorial inputs, while representing real labour and skill, were essentially mechanical, non-judgmental, and therefore insufficiently original; but its paragraph-numbering/internal-referencing scheme and its editorial characterisation of judicial opinions (concurring/dissenting, etc.) did involve genuine legal skill and judgment, and copyright was recognised in those specific inputs.

### 4.3 The Court's Answers to the Legal Questions

**On Question 1 (standard of originality for a derivative work):** The Supreme Court held that the applicable standard in India is the **"skill and judgment" standard** articulated in *CCH Canadian Ltd. v. Law Society of Upper Canada* — a derivative work must originate from the author (not be copied from another work) and must be the product of the exercise of the author's skill and judgment; that exercise must not be so trivial that it can be characterised as a purely mechanical exercise. This standard rejects the "sweat of the brow" doctrine (too low, rewarding mere labour and capital) but does not require a novel or non-obvious "creativity" in the patent-law sense (too high). As the Court put it, to claim copyright in a compilation, "the author must produce the material with exercise of his skill and judgment which may not be creativity in the sense that it is novel or non-obvious, but at the same time it is not a product of merely labour and capital" (para 38).

**On Question 2 (whether the copy-edited SCC judgments as a whole attract copyright):** The Court held that the copy-edited version of the judgments, taken **as a whole**, does **not** attract a fresh copyright merely by virtue of the skill, labour and capital that EBC invested in compiling it, because "original or innovative thoughts are... completely excluded" from most of the individual inputs. However, the Court did **not** accept the "all or nothing" whole-work approach urged by EBC; instead it dissected the specific inputs and held that **certain individual inputs** — namely, (i) the breaking up of the judgment's running text into separate, numbered paragraphs together with internal cross-referencing, and (ii) the addition of characterisations of judicial opinions (concurring, partly concurring, partly dissenting, dissenting, supplementing, etc.) — did meet the requisite "skill and judgment" threshold, since they require the editor to understand legal argumentation, the structure of judicial reasoning, and the substantive disagreement or agreement between judges. Copyright was recognised in EBC's favour specifically in respect of these two categories of inputs, and not in respect of the copy-edited judgment text as an undifferentiated whole.

---

## 5. Decision of the Court

The Supreme Court **partly allowed** the appeals. It:

1. **Affirmed** the interim relief already granted by the Delhi High Court, under which the respondents remain entitled to sell their CD-ROMs containing the text of Supreme Court judgments together with their own headnotes and editorial notes, provided such headnotes and editorial notes are not copied from EBC's; and the respondents were restrained from copying the footnotes and editorial notes appearing in EBC's journal.
2. **Additionally directed** that the respondents shall not use (a) the paragraph numbering created by EBC in its copy-edited version for internal cross-referencing, or (b) EBC's editorial characterisations of judges' opinions (e.g., "partly concurring," "partly dissenting") as reflected in judgments reported in SCC.
3. Held that, in view of this decision on the civil appeals, it was **unnecessary to pass any order on the contempt petition**, which was accordingly disposed of.
4. Ordered **no costs**.

## 6. Legal Implications: Why This Is a Landmark Judgment in Indian Copyright Law

- **Adoption of the "skill and judgment" standard of originality.** This judgment is the leading Indian authority establishing that originality for copyright purposes in a derivative work is judged neither by the low-threshold "sweat of the brow" doctrine (mere labour and capital) nor by a high-threshold "creativity"/novelty standard borrowed from patent law, but by an intermediate test: the work must originate from the author and be the product of the exercise of skill and judgment that is more than a purely mechanical exercise.
- **Public-domain status of judicial pronouncements reaffirmed.** By reading Sections 2(k), 17(d) and 52(1)(q)(iv) together, the Court reaffirmed that judgments of courts are "government work," that the Government is the presumptive first owner of copyright in them, and that their reproduction or publication (unless prohibited) can never be an infringement — meaning no publisher can ever acquire a monopoly over the underlying text of a judgment merely by being first to publish it.
- **A granular, component-based approach to compilations.** Rather than treating a compiled/edited work as an indivisible whole that either wholly qualifies or wholly fails for copyright (as some of the English authorities like *Ladbroke* suggested), the Court adopted a dissection-based approach — examining each category of editorial input separately to determine whether it individually meets the originality threshold. This allows copyright to attach selectively to specific creative contributions within a larger derivative work, even where the work as a whole does not qualify.
- **Distinction between mechanical/formatting inputs and judgment-requiring inputs.** The judgment draws a workable line between (a) inputs that are essentially clerical, formatting, or fact-verification in nature (cross-citations, corrected case names, punctuation, abbreviations, margin headings, page references) — which do not meet the originality threshold — and (b) inputs that require substantive legal understanding and discernment (paragraph segmentation reflecting the structure of legal argument, and characterisation of concurrence/dissent among judges) — which do.
- **Harmonisation of Indian law with comparative copyright jurisprudence.** The decision situates Indian copyright doctrine within the global debate on originality, expressly aligning it with the Canadian Supreme Court's *CCH Canadian* formulation over both the historical English "sweat of the brow" approach and the American "minimal creativity" standard from *Feist Publications* — making this the definitive Indian precedent for assessing originality in factual compilations and derivative works generally, well beyond the specific context of law reporting.
