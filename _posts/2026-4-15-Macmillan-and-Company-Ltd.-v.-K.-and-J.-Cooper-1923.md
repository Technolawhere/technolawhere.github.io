# Macmillan and Company Ltd. v. K. and J. Cooper (1923): Copyright in Selections and Compilations from Non-Copyright Works

**Citation:** MANU/PR/0118/1923, AIR 1924 PC 75
**Court:** Privy Council
**Decided On:** 14 December 1923
**Bench:** Atkinson, Lord Sumner (Louis Addin Kershaw), Wrenbury, Carson and Robert Stout, JJ.
**Disposition:** Appeal Allowed

---

## 1. One-Minute Summary

Macmillan and Company published a schoolbook consisting of extracts taken verbatim from Sir Thomas North's translation of Plutarch's *Life of Alexander* — a work that was itself out of copyright. The extracts were stitched together with brief transitional notes, and the book also carried marginal notes, an introduction, a chronological table, and explanatory notes and a glossary. K. and J. Cooper later published a rival book built on the same plan: passages selected from North's translation (including all of Macmillan's selections plus more), joined by connecting words, and — crucially — notes that closely followed Macmillan's own notes. Macmillan sued for infringement of copyright in its book.

The Privy Council held that **merely selecting and reprinting verbatim passages from a non-copyright work, without condensing or reshaping them, does not require sufficient knowledge, labour, judgment or literary skill to attract copyright** in the text itself. However, the **notes, introduction, and other original apparatus added by Macmillan did involve sufficient skill, labour and judgment**, and were therefore protected by copyright. Since Cooper had substantially and serially copied those notes, Cooper was held liable for infringement — but only in respect of the notes and glossary, not the main text.

The ratio turns on the distinction between the "materials" (North's translation, which was public property) and the "product" of an author's skill, labour and judgment applied to those materials. Copyright protects only the latter, and the degree of skill, labour and judgment required is a question of fact and degree in each case. The judgment is a landmark because it authoritatively distinguishes true "abridgments" from mere reprints of selected passages, and because it lays down the enduring test that originality in copyright law relates to the *expression* of thought — not to the novelty of the underlying ideas or materials.

## 3. Facts and Background

* The appellants, Macmillan and Company Ltd., published a schoolbook titled *"Plutarch's Life of Alexander. Sir Thomas North's Translation. Edited for Schools by H.W.M. Parr, M.A."*
* North's translation itself was **not protected by copyright** (it was old and in the public domain), and the Board proceeded on that footing without deciding the more difficult question of whether North's translation could, in principle, ever have enjoyed copyright.
* Macmillan's book took roughly 20,000 of North's original 10,000 [sic, as per the judgment's figures] words — selected, non-contiguous passages — and joined them with a few connecting words to create the appearance of continuous narrative. The passages themselves were reprinted verbatim, without condensation or modification.
* In addition to this text, Macmillan's book contained substantial original apparatus: marginal notes, an introduction on North's translation and Alexander's place in history, an analysis of the book's contents, a chronological table of Alexander's life, short "transition notes" bridging omitted passages, and appended notes and a glossary. The book was divided into six chapters.
* Macmillan's book was prescribed by the Bombay University Syndicate (notice dated 14 October 1917) as a set text for the 1919 Matriculation Examination.
* The respondents, K. and J. Cooper, subsequently published a rival book, *"Plutarch's Life of Alexander the Great. North's Translation, edited with Introduction, Marginalia, Notes and Summary by A. Darby, M.A."* Cooper had advertised this book, in a handbill, as intended to compete for the same Bombay Matriculation market, claiming their editions would be "more reliable" and "more useful" than similar editions from England.
* Cooper's book followed exactly the same general plan: selected passages from North's translation joined by connecting words. Cooper's text **included every passage found in Macmillan's book, plus 7,000 additional words** from North's translation. Cooper omitted Macmillan's transitional notes and side-notes, but the notes it did include were, in many instances, **serially copied** from Macmillan's notes.
* The Court of Appeal below found — and the Privy Council agreed — that Cooper intended to produce a book students would buy in preference to Macmillan's, and that the respondents' evidence to the contrary was not credible.
* Macmillan sued for a declaration that Cooper's book infringed its copyright, along with an injunction, damages and other relief.

---

## 4. Legal Issues

The Board framed the central question as follows:

1. **Whether the appellants (Macmillan) were entitled to copyright in the text of their book** — i.e., the selected and rearranged passages from North's translation.
2. **Whether the appellants were entitled to copyright in the notes and other original apparatus** attached to their book (introduction, marginal notes, transitional notes, chronological table, appended notes and glossary).
3. As a consequence of (1) and (2): **whether the respondents' book, which reproduced the same textual passages and copied many of the notes, constituted infringement.**

A subsidiary question raised in argument — whether North's translation itself was or could be the subject of copyright — was expressly left undecided, since it did not arise on the facts as the Board found them.

---

## 5. Analysis of Each Legal Issue

### Issue 1: Was there copyright in the *text* — the selected passages from North's translation?

**Appellants' argument:** Macmillan contended that the selection, compression, and arrangement of passages from North's 10,000-word translation into a coherent 20,000-word narrative required sufficient skill and judgment to constitute an original literary work.

**Court's reasoning:** The Board rejected this. It first clarified that what Macmillan and Cooper had each produced was not a true "abridgment" at all. Drawing on Copinger's *Law of Copyright* (1904 and 1915 editions), the Board explained that a genuine abridgment requires the abridger to *restate* the substance of the original work in **substantially different, condensed language** — an exercise of independent labour and literary skill. Simply selecting scraps of an author's own words and stringing them together, without paraphrase or condensation, does not meet this standard; "the reduction of the size and work by copying some of its parts and omitting others confers no title to authorship."

The Board held that Macmillan's passages were reprinted **in their original form**, "not condensed, expanded, modified or reshaped to any extent whatever." No material change of scientific knowledge, sound judgment, literary skill or taste was needed to select passages that were merely inoffensive for schoolchildren or of manageable length. Therefore, **on the facts, there was no evidence of sufficient labour, skill or judgment applied to the text itself** to entitle Macmillan to copyright in it.

Importantly, the Board did **not** lay down a categorical rule that a compilation of selected passages from another work can *never* attract copyright. It expressly disagreed with the appellate court below on this point, giving the example of a scientific anthology where the passages are so interdependent that selecting and arranging them for educational use would itself require real skill, knowledge and judgment — in such a case, copyright could well subsist in the selection. The outcome here simply turned on the *absence* of such skill on the facts.

**Ratio on this issue:** Copyright does not protect the raw materials (here, North's public-domain translation) but only the *product* of an author's skill, labour and judgment applied to those materials. Where selection and arrangement of pre-existing verbatim text require no more than trivial effort, no copyright arises in that text, however useful the resulting compilation may be.

### Issue 2: Was there copyright in the notes and other original apparatus?

**Court's reasoning:** The Board reached the opposite conclusion regarding Macmillan's notes. It found that the notes were "well chosen," "neatly condensed," "sufficiently copious," and accurate, and that framing them required classical knowledge, literary skill and taste, labour, and sound judgment as to what would be useful for schoolboys preparing for a matriculation examination. The respondents' argument that the notes were "useless" because their content could be found in Lempriere's *Classical Dictionary* was dismissed — the Board observed that having the relevant information conveniently at hand, rather than requiring students to consult a large reference dictionary, was itself a real value added by the compiler's labour.

The Board relied on **Lord Kinloch's judgment in *Black v. Murray* (1870) 9 Ret 341**, which held that copyright in a new edition of an out-of-copyright work is not created by trivial emendations or "a few unimportant notes," but *is* created where the added notes are "extensive and substantial," impart "a true and real value over and above that belonging to the text," and reflect a genuine exercise of intellect and learning. Applying this standard, the Board found Macmillan's notes crossed the threshold into copyrightable original work.

Since Cooper's book had, "in many instances," **serially copied** Macmillan's notes — a fact the Board considered could only be explained by direct copying, given the "absolute verbal identity" of some passages — Cooper was held to have infringed Macmillan's copyright **in the notes and glossary**, though not in the text.

**Ratio on this issue:** Original notes, introductions, and similar explanatory apparatus added to a non-copyright text can independently attract copyright, provided they reflect genuine knowledge, labour, judgment and literary skill, even though the underlying text they annotate is itself unprotected.

### Precedents Relied Upon and Their Treatment

| Precedent | Principle | How Applied by the Board |
|---|---|---|
| **Walter v. Lane [1900] A.C. 539** | A person who transcribes and publishes a verbatim report of a public speech is the "author" of that report and entitled to copyright in it, because of the skill, labour and expense involved in producing the report; copyright protects the *product* of labour and skill, not the raw underlying material (the speech itself). Lord Halsbury's judgment invoked the moral basis of copyright as resting on the principle "Thou shalt not steal." | **Followed and central to the reasoning.** The Board used this case as the foundation for the "materials vs. product" distinction that drives the entire judgment — copyright protects the fruits of one's own skill, labour and capital, not the raw material on which it is expended. |
| **Macmillan v. Suresh Chunder Deb (1890) I.L.R. 17 Cal. 951** | Sir Arthur Wilson held that copyright can subsist in a *selection* of poems (Palgrave's "Golden Treasury of Songs and Lyrics") compiled from works whose individual copyrights had lapsed, because compiling such a selection required extensive reading, careful study, comparison, and the exercise of taste and judgment. | **Approved.** The Board went out of its way to correct a misreading of this case advanced by Macmillan's counsel (that Sir Arthur Wilson had not actually decided the existence of copyright in the selection) and confirmed that the case squarely established that copyright can exist in a well-crafted selection, distinguishing it from Macmillan's own text, which lacked comparable skill. |
| **University of London Press, Limited v. University Tutorial Press, Limited [1916] 2 Ch. 601** | Peterson J. held that the word "original" in "original literary work" (Copyright Act 1911, s.1(1)) does not require originality of *thought*; it requires only that the *expression* of thought not be copied from another work, but originate from the author. | **Approved and adopted verbatim as the correct construction of Section 1(1).** This became the Board's own test for originality, applied throughout the judgment. |
| **Emerson v. Davies (1843) 3 Story 768 (US)** | Story J. held that a new and original plan, arrangement or combination of materials attracts copyright even if the underlying materials are old, provided the combination itself has not been used before for the same or any purpose; borrowing hints or materials from existing sources does not preclude copyright in one's own arrangement. | **Treated as persuasive** (not binding, but "sound, able, convincing and helpful"). Reinforced the Board's central distinction between materials and the product of skill, labour and learning applied to them. |
| **Hogg v. Scott (1874) L.R. 18 Eq. 444** (per Hall V.C., quoted within *Macmillan v. Suresh Chunder Deb*) | A defendant is not entitled to avail himself of the labour the plaintiff expended in producing a work — to do so is simply to appropriate the result of another's labour. | Cited via Sir Arthur Wilson's judgment as the guiding principle applied to selections, maps, guidebooks, directories and dictionaries, and, by extension, to Macmillan's notes. |
| **Longman v. Winchester (1809) 16 Ves. 209** (Lord Eldon), approved in **Spiers v. Brown (1858) 6 W.R. Eng. 352** | Two compilers may independently make the same selection from original authors, but one may not take advantage of a selection already made by another — he must go back to the original sources himself. | Quoted approvingly (via *Suresh Chunder Deb*) as underpinning the rule that copying another's selection, rather than independently compiling one's own, is an infringement. |
| **Moffatt & Paige, Ltd. v. George Gill & Sons, Ltd. (1902) 86 L.T. 465** (Collins M.R.) | Approved and applied Sir Arthur Wilson's reasoning in the Golden Treasury case in a similar dispute over reproduction of a compiled selection with added notes. | Cited to demonstrate that the Court of Appeal had already endorsed *Suresh Chunder Deb* as a sound decision, reinforcing its authority for the Privy Council. |
| **Black v. Murray (1870) 9 Ret 341** (Lord Kinloch) | Copyright in a new edition of an out-of-copyright work is not created by trivial textual emendations or unimportant notes, but *is* created where added notes are extensive, substantial, and impart real value beyond the original text, reflecting genuine intellectual exercise. | **Directly applied** to hold that Macmillan's notes, being substantial and valuable rather than superficial, attracted copyright — while the bare, unaltered reprint of North's text did not. |
| **Jarrold v. Houlston (1857) 3 K. & J. 708** (Page Wood V.C.) | Collecting and systematically organising commonly asked questions and answers on a scientific subject into instructional form, even where the underlying facts are drawn from general reading or existing works, is sufficient to constitute an original work meriting copyright protection. | Cited as an illustration of how compiling and structuring material for an instructional purpose can itself amount to sufficient original labour — supporting, by analogy, the Board's approach to Macmillan's notes and its earlier acknowledgment that selections of scientific material could attract copyright. |
| **Lamb v. Evans [1893] 1 Ch. 218** | Referenced (via Copinger) among the authorities supporting the propositions on abridgment and originality. | Cited generally in support of the Copinger passages on abridgment; not analysed independently. |

### The Question of Copyright in North's Translation Itself

The Board noted that considerable argument had been directed at whether North's translation of Plutarch was, or could become, the subject of copyright. The Board expressly **declined to decide this question**, holding that it did not arise on the facts, since it proceeded on the footing (undisputed on the record) that North's translation did not then enjoy copyright protection.

---

## 6. Decision

The Privy Council **allowed the appeal**. Their Lordships humbly advised that:

* The decree of the High Court in its appellate jurisdiction, dated 13 October 1921, should be **set aside**, with costs.
* The original decree of Mr. Justice Fawcett, dated 10 March 1921, should be **amended** to restrict the injunction to the **notes and glossary printed at pages 83 to 94** of Macmillan's book (and any passages therefrom), rather than the book as a whole — reflecting the finding that copyright subsisted only in the notes, not in the text at pages 1–82.
* The respondents were directed to pay the appellants' costs of the hearing before Mr. Justice Fawcett.
* Subject to these amendments, the trial court's decree was affirmed.
* **Each party was to bear its own costs of the appeal** before the Privy Council.

## 7. Legal Significance

Macmillan v. Cooper remains a foundational authority in Indian and Commonwealth copyright jurisprudence for several enduring propositions:

* **The "materials versus product" distinction.** Copyright never protects the raw materials an author works upon — only the product of the labour, skill and judgment applied to those materials, sufficient to impart to that product "some quality or character which the raw material did not possess."

* **Originality means expression, not novelty of thought.** Adopting Peterson J.'s formulation from *University of London Press*, the Board confirmed that copyright law is concerned with the *expression* of thought, not the originality of the underlying ideas — a work is "original" if it is not copied from another and originates from its author, regardless of whether its content is itself novel.

* **The threshold is a question of degree, not a bright-line rule.** The precise quantum of "knowledge, labour, judgment or literary skill or taste" required to earn copyright "cannot be defined in precise terms" and depends on the facts of each case — a flexible, fact-sensitive standard that later Indian courts have repeatedly invoked in assessing originality thresholds.

* **Selections and compilations can be independently copyrightable**, distinct from the underlying (even non-copyright) source material, provided real skill, judgment and labour go into the selection or arrangement — as affirmed through the Board's endorsement of *Macmillan v. Suresh Chunder Deb*.

* **Verbatim reproduction of passages, however cleverly stitched together, is not itself sufficient** to found copyright, and is distinguished from a true "abridgment," which requires restatement in substantially different language reflecting independent labour.

* **Annotations and editorial apparatus on a public-domain text can attract their own, freestanding copyright**, separate from any claim over the underlying text — a principle of continuing relevance to editors, annotators and publishers of critical or educational editions of out-of-copyright works.

These principles — particularly the materials/product distinction and the "expression, not ideas" formulation of originality — have had lasting influence on the development of Indian copyright law's approach to compilations, derivative works, and the idea–expression dichotomy.
