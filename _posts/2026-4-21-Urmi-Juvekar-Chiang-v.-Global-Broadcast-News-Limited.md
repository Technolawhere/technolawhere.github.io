# Urmi Juvekar Chiang v. Global Broadcast News Limited (Bombay High Court, 2007)

**Court:** High Court of Bombay
**Coram:** A.M. Khanwilkar, J.
**Case:** Notice of motion No. (sic) of 007 in Suit (Lodging) No. 1467 of 2007
**Decided on:** 07.06.2007

---

## 1. One-Minute Summary

The Plaintiff, a script-writer, conceived a reality television format titled *"Work in Progress"*: a show following individual citizens across different cities as they tried to resolve a civic problem of their choice. She reduced this idea to a detailed concept note and production plan, registered the concept note with the Film Writers' Association, Mumbai, and shared it in confidence with the Defendant broadcaster over an extended period of negotiations. When the Defendant did not respond further and after it launched a strikingly similar show, *"Summer Showdown"*, on its news channel, the Plaintiff sought ad-interim injunctive relief on two independent grounds: **breach of confidence** and **infringement of copyright** in her literary work (the concept note and production plan).

The Court held that:

- **Breach of confidence** is a broader remedy than copyright and protects ideas/concepts disclosed in confidence, even though there is no copyright in an idea per se. Applying the four-part test culled out in *Zee Telefilms Ltd. v. Sundial Communication Pvt. Ltd.* (relying on *CMI Centers for Medical Innovation GMBH v. Phytopharm PLC*), the Plaintiff made out a strong prima facie case for breach of confidence.
- **Copyright infringement**: While there is no copyright in an idea, theme, or plot, copyright subsists in the *form of expression* of that idea, including the selection and arrangement of incidents (format, treatment, structure), as set out in the Plaintiff's concept note and production plan, which constituted "literary work" under Section 2(o) of the Copyright Act, 1957. Applying the "average viewer" test from *R.G. Anand v. Delux Films* rather than a hypercritical, line-by-line comparison, the Court found that the Defendant's programme had substantially lifted the format, treatment, and structure of the Plaintiff's work; the differences pointed out by the Defendant were merely cosmetic/trivial.
- The Court granted ad-interim relief restraining the Defendant from telecasting/exploiting "Summer Showdown" insofar as it infringed the Plaintiff's copyright and breached her confidence, pending final disposal of the Notice of Motion.

**Key ratio decidendi / legal takeaways:**

1. There is a clear conceptual distinction between the law of confidence and the law of copyright — confidence is a broader, non-proprietary right that can protect a mere idea or concept, whereas copyright never subsists in an idea, only in its expression.
2. A four-fold test governs breach-of-confidence claims: (i) clear identification of the information relied upon; (ii) that it was imparted in circumstances of confidence; (iii) that it was information of a type capable of being treated as confidential; and (iv) that it was used without licence, or there was a threat to use it. At the interlocutory stage, the plaintiff need only show a seriously arguable case on limbs (iii) and (iv).
3. The format, treatment, structure and sequencing of a television programme, once reduced to a concept note/production plan, is a "literary work" and can attract copyright protection, even though the underlying idea (e.g., "citizen activism on civic issues") is not protectable.
4. The test for infringement is not hypercritical, meticulous, line-by-line dissection, but the impression of an average viewer/spectator upon viewing both works (the *R.G. Anand v. Delux Films* propositions were applied).
5. Cosmetic or superficial differences (e.g., four individuals vs five families, daily capsule vs weekly episode) will not defeat a claim of infringement where the substantial format and treatment have been lifted.

## 3. Facts and Background of the Case

- In or about November 2005, the Plaintiff, a reputed script-writer, conceived the idea of a reality television programme that would follow citizens from different parts of the country as they took the initiative to solve a civic problem of their choice in their locality, highlighting their fight against bureaucracy, conflicting interests, and destructive attitudes, even where they ultimately failed to solve the problem.
- The Plaintiff developed this idea into a detailed concept note (containing the concept, form, treatment, problems, etc.), titled *"Work in Progress"*, and got it registered with the Film Writers' Association, Mumbai, on 9 November 2005.
- Beginning March 2006, the Plaintiff approached the Defendant (through Ms. Rasika Tyagi) and shared the concept with her; Ms. Tyagi responded that the concept "sounds interesting" and invited further discussion.
- The Plaintiff engaged a producer (Mr. Arjun Gaurisaria) and made a detailed presentation of her further developed concept note and production plan to the Defendant. Budget negotiations followed over several months (2006–early 2007), including further emails to Ms. Tyagi and to Mr. Rajdeep Sardesai (Editor-in-Chief of the Defendant), and involvement of Mr. Ramchandran Srinivasan (an Executive Producer known to Mr. Sardesai).
- Despite repeated follow-ups (including as late as 14 April 2007), the Defendant did not respond substantively.
- On 19 May 2007, the Plaintiff discovered that the Defendant had launched a television programme titled *"Summer Showdown"* on its CNN-IBN channel (already airing since 14 May 2007), showcasing five families across five cities trying to find solutions to civic problems arising from summer conditions.
- The Plaintiff alleged that "Summer Showdown" substantially reproduced the format, treatment, structure and expression of her concept for "Work in Progress" with only cosmetic changes (e.g., five families instead of four individual protagonists) made to the entire concept and that this was done using confidential information she had shared with the Defendant, without licence.
- The Plaintiff filed suit alleging (a) breach of confidentiality, and (b) infringement of copyright in her literary work (concept notes and production plan), and sought ad-interim relief.
- Notably, when the Notice of Motion was first moved before the Vacation Judge on 23 May 2007, the Defendant's counsel represented that the programme was merely a "news clipping" and a "live" programme (not a reality show based on the Plaintiff's concept). The Defendant later sought to have this statement corrected/clarified on 24 May 2007, but the Vacation Judge rejected the request, finding no ambiguity in the earlier order requiring correction.
- The Defendant, in its reply affidavit, set out a tabulated list of dissimilarities between the two shows; the Plaintiff, in rejoinder, set out a tabulated list of striking similarities.

---

## 4. Legal Questions Before the Court

1. Whether the Plaintiff made out a case for grant of ad-interim relief on the ground of **breach of confidentiality**?
2. Whether the Plaintiff made out a case for grant of ad-interim relief on the ground of **infringement of copyright** in her literary work?
3. What is the correct standard/test to be applied by a court, at the interlocutory stage, in comparing two works to determine whether infringement (of confidence or copyright) has occurred?

---

## 5. Discussion on Each Legal Question

### 5.1 Whether the Plaintiff made out a case for breach of confidentiality

**Arguments:**
- The Defendant argued that no confidentiality could be claimed over the subject-matter of "civic woes," since this was already in the public domain, and that breach of confidence could only be invoked in relation to use of a script, characterisation, sequences, or dialogues.
- The Plaintiff argued that her claim was not to confidentiality in the general subject of civic problems, but specifically to "her concept" of the programme, and to the concept notes and production plan detailing the format, treatment, problems, and manner of spreading awareness, all of which were shared with the Defendant under a clear mutual understanding that the Defendant would either accept or reject the proposal, but not use it without licence.

**Development of Reasoning:**

The Court relied heavily on the Division Bench decision of the Bombay High Court in **Zee Telefilms Ltd. and Anr. v. Sundial Communication Pvt. Ltd. and Ors.**, (2003(5) Bom.C.R.404), which had held that the law of confidence is distinct from the law of copyright. The court there had held that the right to restrain publication on the ground of breach of trust or confidence is a *broader* right than the proprietary right of copyright. There is no copyright in ideas or information, and it is not an infringement of copyright to adopt the ideas of another or publish information received from another (absent substantial copying of form). **However**, if the ideas or information were acquired under circumstances making it a breach of good faith to publish them, an injunction may still be granted for breach of confidence.

From *Zee Telefilms* (which in turn relied on **CMI Centers for Medical Innovation GMBH and Anr. v. Phytopharm PLC**, [1999] Fleet Street Reports 235), the Court extracted the **four-part test** for an action of breach of confidence:

1. The Plaintiff must clearly identify the information relied upon.
2. The Plaintiff must show it was handed over in circumstances of confidence.
3. The Plaintiff must show it was information of a type which could be treated as confidential.
4. The Plaintiff must show it was used without licence, or there was a threat to use it.

Crucially, the Court noted that **at the interlocutory stage**, the Plaintiff does not need to conclusively *prove* limbs (3) and (4) as she would at trial. It is sufficient that she addresses them and demonstrates at least a **seriously arguable case**.

The Court also drew on:
- ***Fraser v. Thames Television Ltd.***, [1983(2) All.E.R. 101]: Here breach of confidence was claimed in relation to an idea for a television series disclosed *orally* and in confidence; the Court there held that a recipient of such an idea could be restrained from disclosing/using it for an unlimited period, or until the idea becomes general public knowledge. The Bombay High Court used this to reject the Defendant's argument that confidentiality could not attach to a mere "idea."
- ***Anil Gupta and Anr. v. Kunal Das Gupta and Ors.*** (Delhi High Court), [2002 (97) Delhi Law Times 257]: This case itself relied on *Talbot v. General Television Corporation Pvt. Ltd.* [1981 R.P.C. 1] and the treatise "Law of Trade Secrets" by Robert Dean, on the meaning of "novel" (previously unknown, or inventive/unique). *Anil Gupta* had also imported the "springboard doctrine" from ***Terrapin v. Builder Supply Company***: namely, that a person who has obtained information in confidence cannot use it as a "springboard" for activities detrimental to the confider, and this restraint continues even after the features become public or ascertainable by inspection. The court in this case also observed that concepts/ideas for reality TV shows given to a broadcaster deserve protection, since broadcasters with greater resources could otherwise appropriate an individual creator's idea and leave the originator "high and dry."
- ***Midas Hygiene Industries P. Ltd. and Anr. v. Sudhir Bhatia and Ors.***, [2004 (28) PTC 121 (SC)]: The Supreme Court, in this case, had observed that in cases of infringement of trademark or copyright, an injunction must normally follow; mere delay does not defeat the grant of injunction, and injunction becomes necessary where the adoption itself was prima facie dishonest. The Bombay High Court used this to reinforce that if the Defendant misused confidential information without licence, the conduct would necessarily be dishonest, warranting injunction.

**Application:**

The Court found:
- The Plaintiff had clearly identified the information relied upon, i.e., her concept and concept notes for "Work in Progress." (thus, Limb 1 of the 4-fold-test satisfied)
- The contemporaneous e-mail record (spanning March 2006 to April 2007) clearly showed that the information was passed on in circumstances of confidence, with a shared understanding that the Defendant would accept or reject the proposal but not use it independently. (thus, Limb 2 of the 4-fold-test satisfied)
- The information (format, treatment, production plan) was of a type capable of being treated as confidential, notwithstanding that the general subject-matter of "civic woes" was in the public domain. There was some application of the human brain that gave it confidential character (see Anil Gupta). Confidentiality was claimed in the concept and manner of treatment, not in the underlying civic issue itself. (thus, Limb 3 of the 4-fold-test satisfied)
- There was no dispute that the Defendant had received the information; the Defendant did not plead that the information was not handed over in confidence, nor did it produce any affidavit from Ms. Tyagi or Mr. Sardesai to rebut the Plaintiff's version. This lead the Court to draw an adverse inference against the Defendant.
- There was obvious use of the information with only cosmetic changes made to it (see next section). (thus, Limb 4 of the 4-fold-test satisfied)

The Court concluded that the Plaintiff had made out "more than a seriously arguable case" on breach of confidentiality and was entitled to ad-interim relief on this ground.

### 5.2 Whether the Plaintiff made out a case for infringement of copyright

**Arguments:**
- The Defendant argued that the Plaintiff's grievance was, at best, one relating to an abstract idea or concept (citizen activism towards civic problems), which is not entitled to copyright protection; it also argued (in relation to a comparison of a "clock" motif used in both shows) that any similarity in a "clock" device would, at best, amount to infringement of an artistic work, not a literary work.
- The Plaintiff argued that her grievance was not one of mere idea infringement, but of substantial reproduction/adaptation of her literary work (the concept notes and production plan, which detailed the format, treatment, problems, and structure of the programme).

**Development of Reasoning:**

The Court referred to **Copinger and Skone James on Copyright**, 14th edition:
- Para 2-05: Copyright law is concerned with preventing copying of *material form*, not with reproduction of ideas. Ideas are "free as air." If an idea is worked out in detail and the defendant reproduces that detailed expression, infringement may arise even though it is not the idea itself that is protected but its detailed expression.
- Para 3-10: The "form of expression" of a literary work is not confined to the text. It may include the selection and arrangement, in a particular order, of incidents, whether factual or fictional. Even works whose principal purpose is to communicate "news" are not for that reason excluded from copyright protection. What is protected is the form of expression, not the underlying information.

The Court then applied the leading Supreme Court authority on the subject, ***R.G. Anand v. Delux Films and Ors.***, [AIR 1978 SC 1613], extracting the seven propositions from Paragraph 46 of that judgment, including (in summary):

1. There is no copyright in an idea, subject-matter, theme, plot, or historical/legendary facts. Infringement is confined to the form, manner, arrangement, and expression of the idea.
2. Where the same idea is developed differently, similarities may naturally occur from a common source; the court must determine whether the similarities are on fundamental/substantial aspects of the mode of expression. A literal imitation with minor variations amounts to violation; the copying must be substantial and material to constitute piracy.
3. The surest test is whether an average reader/spectator/viewer, after experiencing both works, is left with the unmistakable impression that the subsequent work is a copy of the original.
4. Where the theme is the same but presented and treated differently so as to become a new work, there is no infringement.
5. Material and broad dissimilarities that negative an intention to copy, with incidental coincidences, will not amount to infringement.
6. Infringement, being akin to piracy, must be proved by clear and cogent evidence.
7. In relation to films specifically, the task of the plaintiff is harder because of the film medium's broader scope, but if the viewer's totality impression is that the film is largely a copy, infringement may be proved.

The Court also referred to ***Sheldon v. Metro Goldwyn Pictures Corporation***, [1993 (81) F 2nd 49] (cited via *Zee Telefilms*) for the proposition that it is enough that "substantial parts were lifted; no playwright can excuse wrong by showing how much of his work he did not pirate," i.e., pointing to differences does not exculpate a defendant if the substantial part has in fact been copied.

The Defendant relied on ***Star India Pvt. Ltd. v. Leo Burnett (India) Pvt. Ltd.***, [2003 (27) PTC 81 (Bom.)], where a narrower copyright protection had been applied because that case concerned films/sound recordings (for which originality is not a precondition), as distinguished from literary, dramatic or artistic works (which do require originality). The Bombay High Court distinguished this authority on the basis that the present dispute concerned a literary work (the concept note/production plan), not a film or sound recording, and hence attracted the wider protection applicable to literary works.


**Application:**

The Court compared the two tabulated lists placed on record: the Defendant's list of "dissimilarities" and the Plaintiff's list of "striking similarities" and held that the differences highlighted by the Defendant (e.g., five families instead of four individuals, summer-specific problems, no dedicated screening criteria beyond facing a civic problem) were "only cosmetic" changes and did not detract from the fact that the substantial format, treatment, and structure of the Plaintiff's work had been lifted. Applying the "average viewer" standard rather than a hypercritical, meticulous comparison, the Court found that an average viewer, upon seeing both works, would get the unmistakable impression that "Summer Showdown" was based on or taken from "Work in Progress."

The Court also rejected the Defendant's contention that it had independently conceived "Summer Showdown" in-house around March–April 2007 as a show about "summer-related problems," noting that even on the Defendant's own case, any subsequent "modification" of that in-house idea ought to have remained confined to the summer theme, whereas what was eventually aired bore the format and treatment of the Plaintiff's "Work in Progress" concept. The Defendant's separate argument that it had already been running an unrelated show titled "Citizen Journalist" since December 2005 was also held to be of no assistance, given the finding of striking similarity with the Plaintiff's specific work.

The Court further noted that the Defendant had not filed affidavits from its own key representatives (Ms. Rasika Tyagi or Mr. Rajdeep Sardesai) to rebut the Plaintiff's detailed, contemporaneous documentary record thus warranting an adverse inference, and reinforcing the credibility of the Plaintiff's version that her proposal had never actually been rejected but was, instead, used without licence.

### 5.3 The correct standard for comparison at the interlocutory stage

The Court expressly held, drawing on *R.G. Anand* and *Zee Telefilms*, that for both breach-of-confidence and copyright-infringement claims, the correct approach is **not** a hypercritical or meticulous, line-by-line scrutiny of the two works, but rather an assessment from the standpoint of the **observations and impressions of an average viewer**. If substantial parts of the plaintiff's work have been lifted, this constitutes infringement, notwithstanding the presence of some genuine or contrived dissimilarities.

---

## 6. Decision of the Court

The Bombay High Court granted **ad-interim relief** in terms of prayer clauses (a) and (b) of the Notice of Motion, restraining the Defendant, its directors, servants, agents, and associate concerns/firms from:

(a) infringing the Plaintiff's copyright in her original literary work (the concept note of "Work in Progress") by making, copying, reproducing, publishing, broadcasting, or otherwise exploiting the same through the making/broadcasting of "Summer Showdown," without the Plaintiff's consent; and

(b) breaching the confidential information imparted by the Plaintiff (contained in the concept note of "Work in Progress") in the same manner, without the Plaintiff's consent/licence.

This relief was to operate until disposal of the Notice of Motion, which was posted for hearing on 23 July 2007. The Court also rejected the Defendant's request for a limited stay of the order pending appeal, noting that the Defendant's programme was, in any event, scheduled to air only until 10 June 2007, and that granting a stay would render the Motion infructuous, whereas refusing it would cause no real prejudice to the Defendant.

## 7. Key Legal Implications for Indian Copyright Law

- **Confidence vs. Copyright: a broader remedy for creative concepts:** The judgment reaffirms and applies the important doctrinal distinction (traced from *Zee Telefilms*) that breach of confidence is a broader, non-proprietary cause of action than copyright, capable of protecting a bare idea or concept disclosed under circumstances of confidentiality even where copyright law itself would not protect the idea as such. This is significant for creators (writers, format developers) who pitch ideas to broadcasters/producers before those ideas are fully reduced to a protectable literary work, or even where they are so reduced but wish to rely on an independent, and often easier, cause of action.
- **A workable four-part test for confidence claims**, with a lowered evidentiary threshold at the interlocutory stage (only "iii" and "iv" need be "seriously arguable," not conclusively proved), giving plaintiffs a realistic route to interim protection against exploitation of pitched concepts.
- **Recognition of TV format/production plans as literary works:** The decision affirms that a detailed concept note and production plan for a reality television show, capturing format, treatment, structure, and sequencing, is a "literary work" under Section 2(o) of the Copyright Act, 1957, separate from the broadcast episodes themselves, and can be infringed by a subsequent programme that substantially reproduces that format/treatment even while dressed up with surface-level changes.
- **Reaffirmation of the idea–expression dichotomy** in the Indian context via Copinger and Skone James and *R.G. Anand v. Delux Films*: there is no copyright in an idea, theme, or plot, but the *detailed working-out* (selection and arrangement of incidents) of that idea is protectable expression.
- **The "average viewer" test, not hypercritical dissection**, is the appropriate standard for comparing two works (whether for confidence or copyright claims). The court must look at overall impression rather than parsing individual differences, and superficial or cosmetic variations will not defeat a claim where the substantial form has been lifted.
- **Adverse inference for withheld testimony:** The judgment illustrates how courts may draw an adverse inference against a defendant that fails to produce affidavits from key personnel involved in the underlying negotiations, particularly where a plaintiff's case is otherwise supported by contemporaneous documentary evidence (emails).
